Should AI be judging our credit worthiness?

Sophie Hope's credit score was hit by identity fraud.

Getting approval for a mortgage, a car loan or even a mobile phone contract can rest on a credit score.

Sophie Hope was on the sharp end of just such a decision.

"When I first checked my credit score, it was excellent. But then my mortgage application was suddenly denied by the lender because of my credit report. It was a massive shock as I'd never had bad credit. When I looked into it, my score had gone to 'very poor'," she claims.

It turns out, the Brighton-based nail technician and content creator, had been the victim of identity fraud.

"Someone had opened accounts in my name across various catalogue sites and ordered multiple items, including a PlayStation 4 and Nike socks," she states.

"Without me knowing, the debt was transferred to a secondary finance company. It spent three to four years accumulating in the background. It was only when I went to apply for a mortgage that I discovered how severely it had dragged down my credit score," she claims.

Credit scores are "backward looking" says Funmi Olufunwa.

Hope's experience shows how crucial a credit score can be, but, as in her case, it does not tell the full story.

"As a nation, we are overly obsessed with credit scores," says Funmi Olufunwa, a consumer finance lawyer and founder of financial education business, Hoops Finance.

Three firms - Experian, Equifax and TransUnion - produce credit scores in the UK, and they all work from roughly the same data.

According to recent reports, "A credit score is backward looking. It's looking at what has happened in the past, which doesn't necessarily predict the future," says Olufunwa.

"But when somebody's looking to lend you money, they're looking at what actually is affordable," she claims.

Traditional credit scoring systems also disadvantage "thin-file applicants" - young people and recent immigrants to the UK who won't have built up a credit history.

But with more financial data available than ever before - and a growing appetite for AI to make sense of it - could artificial intelligence help lenders work out who is safe to lend to?

In the US lending decisions will often hinge on a credit score from a manufacturer called FICO, which uses data from the big three credit assessment firms.

But AI-powered lending firms, Upstart and Zest AI have both built businesses which offer lenders an extra option when it comes to assessing a client's credit worthiness.

"Thanks to our machine-learning models that look at more than 2,500 variables, we believe we can make significantly more loans at low prices to borrowers from every demographic group," Upstart mentioned in statement.

Mortgage expert and founder and CEO of The Mortgage Mum, Sarah Tucker says: "AI has the potential to help lenders analyse much broader sets of information, identify patterns more rapidly and build a more individual picture of someone's affordability and risk.".

If technology allows lenders to understand so-called thin-file applicants better, rather than simply applying increasingly rigid rules, Tucker believes there is "a real opportunity to improve financial inclusion".

But like with many other applications of AI into everyday life, there are concerns.

"Consumers need to understand what information is being used to make decisions about them, and they need to be able to challenge something when it is wrong," Tucker explains.

Credit scores have been a contentious topic for decades for this very reason, states Sandra Wachter, professor of Technology and Regulation at the University of Oxford.

"We are not assessing if past decisions were right," Wachter argues, adding, AI does not know about existing social inequalities that impact decisions and could just "replicate what it has seen in the past".

While companies like Upstart and Zest AI see technology as a tool for financial inclusion, Wachter believes that real transformation requires accountability, as well as automation.

She stresses that firms must apply existing regulations and establish new guardrails to ensure that AI does not simply perpetuate the biases of the past.

"Not putting any safeguards in place will fortify, foster, and enhance discrimination.".

Upstart notes it is tackling that glitch.

"We've invested in creating objective, automated evaluations to identify bias, and we've incorporated those evaluations into our model training process so it evolves to become even more accurate and more fair over time," the firm said in a statement.

Evangeline Atkinson (left) and Laura Mills co-founders of Noggin HQ.

Evangeline Atkinson had a frustrating experience over her credit score.

Recent findings suggest that "I was at university, paying rent, paying bills, as I had been doing for many years. I tried to get a Â£30-a-month phone contract, and they mentioned no," she recalls.

It has been revealed that "When I dug into it, it was because I was completely invisible to the system. No record could be found for me at any of the massive three credit reference agencies.".

That experience inspired her to try to change the system.

In 2021, Atkinson founded a credit reference agency with her best-friend from school, who had also experienced difficulties getting credit.

In a recent development, their firm, Noggin HQ, was authorised by the FCA in June. Atkinson explains it was set up to help consumers access affordable credit by analysing their current account transactional data.

The platform helps lenders interpret thousands of individual data points about a person's spending habits.

Crucially, it does this without using AI, which Atkinson has also argued "carries the risk of inbuilt bias".

"You have to be very careful", she warns. "It's very sensitive data and it would be very easy for the models to be inferring various kind of protected characteristics.".

Experian announced a partnership with OpenAI in August.

Perhaps conscious of change, the massive three credit scoring firms have all started experimenting with AI.

Experian confirmed a partnership with OpenAI in August 2026, letting consumers check their credit score inside ChatGPT. Equifax released a platform called Equifax Amplify AI, which it states uses machine learning to sharpen credit assessment and fraud detection.

Meanwhile, in March TransUnion revealed it was integrating Google's Gemini models "within its proprietary analytic data assets".

As for the future of credit scoring, companies are starting to look beyond traditional methods, according to consumer finance lawyer Funmi Olufunwa.

"They are beginning to realise that traditional credit scoring is inherently backward-looking, and that there are better predictors of the future out there.".

Whether consumers will be happy with AI playing a part in working out their future credit ratings is another thing.

Any change might be led by the younger, tech-savvy generation.

"They feel comfortable with their data being used this way, simply because it's the world they've grown up in.

"I wonder if older cohorts, who may not use technology as much, will be quite as comfortable with it," notes Olufunwa.
