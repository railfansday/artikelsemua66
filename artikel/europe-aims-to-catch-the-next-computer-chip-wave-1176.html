Europe aims to catch the next computer chip wave

Photonic chips use light instead of electrons to process data.

"Photonic chips are on the verge of a breakthrough," states Ton van Mol of the Dutch research institute TNO.

If van Mol is right â and TNO, the European Union and the Dutch government are backing the idea with millions of euros - then the technology could be a gamechanger.

The chips which run today's society, from your phone and TV, to the vast data centres meeting our insatiable desire for AI, are mostly electrical.

They process data by shunting electrons around tiny circuits â a single microchip produced today can contain billions of transistors, and sizes are measured in nanometres (one nanometre is a billionth of a metre).

Because they use light instead of electricity, photonic chips are faster, use less energy and generate less heat than traditional computer chips.

Van Mol hopes for an acceleration in photonics similar to the one seen in electrical chips since the 1970s.

"With optical chips, you are now where electronic chips were maybe 40 [or] 50 years ago.".

American, Taiwanese and Chinese firms dominate the computer chip industry, but Europe sees the emergence of photonic chips as a fresh opportunity to lead, on what could be, a key future technology.

Which is why the European Union has committed â¬400m ($450m; Â£330m) to a project called PIX Europe, external, with the stated goal of making the continent "a global leader in emerging technology for Photonic Integrated Chips".

"Europe mentioned 'we need to have our own manufacturing in-house'," says van Mol, who is leading a key part of the programme.

"We cannot depend like in the past that everything is manufactured in the US or China - that's not wise, so the [European] Chips Act was called into life, and one of the parts of the act is to build pilot lines to really bring the technology, which is now in labs, to the next stage - manufacturing.".

Electronic and photonic chips are produced on wafers made up of hundreds of individual chips. Factories with machines that can handle bigger wafers can churn out chips more efficiently.

"[Photonic chip] manufacturing worldwide is [currently] done on wafers which are four inches (10cm) in size, and the next step is to scale-up production to a six-inch wafer," notes van Mol.

His team are already building a 10,000 sq m factory to produce six-inch indium phosphide wafers on the High Tech Campus in the Dutch city of Eindhoven.

It's due to fully open in 2030 and will be a major step forward, not just for photonics in Europe, but around the world, according to van Mol.

"That's the development TNO is going to do, in a pilot line which is really serious.

"In 2030, we hope that we have a stable, reproducible, high-yield process, and then the intention is that the pilot line [opens] to industrial parties who can make commercial production out of it.".

Netherland's based ASML is a crucial supplier of chip-making equipment.

Europe does have one crucial player in the current computer chip industry - ASML.

The Netherlands-based tech giant is the only tech giant in the world able to build the machines needed to make the most sophisticated semiconductors.

Which is why ASML products will sit at the heart of the pilot line in Eindhoven - it's no coincidence that van Mol's project is based around the corner from ASML's headquarters.

"The way you produce these [photonic] chips is very similar to the way you produce electrical or electronic chip," indicates van Mol.

"You use the same type of equipment, you also need a high-end clean room, you also use wafers, so the production method is very similar to the traditional semiconductor technology.".

ASML itself is keen to explore the opportunities that the emerging technology of photonics could offer.

"Europe has a strong position in photonics research. The next challenge is translating those innovations into industrial-scale manufacturing," notes Stanislas Baron, who is the head of ASML's EU/US Customer Team.

Peter Wennik claims TNO's pilot production line is a "launchpad" for European tech.

ASML's unique success has helped build an influential and lucrative network of companies and institutions devoted to semiconductor technology in and around Eindhoven, and it's a model for what Europe is trying to achieve with photonic chips.

The Eindhoven project has the backing of former ASML chief executive Peter Wennink, author of an influential report for the Dutch government called The Roadmap to Future Prosperity, external, which noted that the market for photonics was expected to grow 10 to 20 times in the next 10 years.

In a news release backing the newly introduced pilot line, Wennink described it as "a launchpad for Europe's future digital economy".

"By investing in scalable production capacity, we are creating an ecosystem that can help shape the global technological direction," he mentioned.

Of course, Europe is not alone in looking to the potential of photonics to earn a global economic advantage.

In June this year, US photonics company Coherent, external unveiled what it called "the world's first and largest volume-production six-inch indium phosphide manufacturing platform" at its plant in Sherman, in Texas.

The expansion is backed by chip manufacturing giant Nvidia, as well as $50 million public funds under the US Chips Act.

It's an illustration of how quickly the market is moving, and how central photonic chips could be to the continuing race to retain an influential stake in AI and technological development more generally.

Europe's bid to establish itself as a global leader in photonic chips â exemplified by the Eindhoven project â is the kind of strategy that makes sense in a globalised economy, notes Joris Teer, policy analyst at the European Institute for Security Studies in Brussels.

If the continent can take a lead in photonic chips, then future developments in AI and data will partly be dependent on Europe.

"Europe knows that its high energy and labour costs, skilled workforce shortages, and lack of private and public capital make an across-the-board reindustrialisation strategy impossible", Teer claims.

"To retain influence, ensuring dependence on European parties rather than European autonomy is key.".

Once up and running, the Eindhoven project will provide a pilot production line which can be used multiple companies.

It will be open to businesses across Europe and beyond, who can design and build chips without having to develop their own expensive production facilities.

"What we want to establish here is what we call a foundry model; there's no intention to have our own product," Ton van Mol notes.

Instead, they will produce to order for outside customers.

It's the same idea which, at huge scale, has brought Taiwanese-based giant TSMC a position of global dominance in the manufacture of microchips.

Photonic chips are already in use, in highly specialised areas in data centres and in Lidar (light detection and ranging) sensor technology, for instance.

For van Mol of TNO, helping the industry to scale up, by easing production challenges and increasing capacity, is the key step to moving them into the tech we use in our daily lives, like phones and laptops, and to drive innovation in areas like medical scanners and 6G communications.

"The demand is there", says van Mol, "and the end goal is to have, in Europe, manufacturing of these chips, which is strategically very important because these chips [could] become the backbone of all data and telecom.".
