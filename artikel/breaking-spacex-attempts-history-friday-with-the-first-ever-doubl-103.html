📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that A little over two years ago, SpaceX launched a Starship into orbit and then caught the Super Heavy booster when it returned to Earth, a feat it has since repeated multiple times. The organization is taking the next step in rocket reusability by catching both the booster and the upper stage of its Starship on Flight 15, something never achieved before.

Industry observers note that spaceX is launching Flight 15 into orbit on Friday, Nov. 13, with the 75-minute launch window opening at 5:45 p.m. ET.

In a recent development, on its mission, the Starship will deploy 26 Starlink V3 satellites, orbit Earth 14 times and return on Saturday. All of that is in line with most of SpaceX’s launches, of which there have been over 280 dating back to 2025.

What makes the launch interesting is that SpaceX is attempting two things it hasn’t attempted before.

The first is catching its latest booster, the Super Heavy V3, using Mechazilla, the informal name SpaceX gave to the launch-and-catch tower located at its facility in Boca Chica, Texas. SpaceX has caught the Super Heavy V1 successfully three times, but this is the first for the V3 rocket. The tower will use its arms like chopsticks to catch the Super Heavy V3 out of midair.

It has been revealed that the second is recovering both stages of the rocket. Prior Starship launches featured just the Super Heavy booster getting caught by Mechazilla while the upper stage landed in the ocean. This time around, SpaceX wants to catch the upper stage when it returns on Saturday with Mechazilla.

“The upcoming flight is planned to be the first to return both Starship and Super Heavy to the launch site for catch,” SpaceX said in a statement. “If successful, this will mark the first full recovery of an orbital launch system and a breakthrough moment in making Starship a reliable, rapidly reusable rocket.”.

SpaceX is streaming the flight from its X account and the company’s website. Coverage starts Friday at 5:15 p.m. ET, 30 minutes before the launch window opens.

The stream should continue through the launch and the Super Heavy V3 catch, expected approximately 6.5 minutes after launch. The organization promises “hours of live views” of Starship as it orbits Earth, offering viewers excellent views of the planet from space.

Sources indicate that spaceX hasn’t specified if it’s going to stream the entire mission, including the second catch when Starship returns to Earth on Saturday, Nov. 14. The expected time of arrival for that landing is 4:17 p.m. ET, assuming no delays or technical problems, which is a little more than 22 hours after launch. SpaceX streams from the same places every time, so if the manufacturer does stream it, the links above will be where to go.

Should the launch be delayed due to weather, technical complications or something else (after all, it is Friday the 13th), SpaceX has not declared a backup launch date at this time.
