Tech Report: Hacks, AI or just overdue updates - why have there been so many outages?

Outages and IT failings are commonplace - but not on this scale.

In a recent development, in the past week, there has been a massive Microsoft outage, an apparent tech glitch which grounded all US flights for hours, and issues for the National Security Agency (NSA). That's not to mention outages affecting the biggest US banks, as well as a major flaw found in a popular programming language.

These are all separate incidents - but the fact they have all come so close to each other has got people wondering if they might be linked.

There is no evidence to suggest this - but they do tell us a lot about how the modern tech world works, and where the vulnerabilities lie.

As is often the case, many outages were a result of an enhancement or installation going wrong somewhere in a system.

A network upgrade at Microsoft caused servers to become overloaded and shut down.

It was also upgrades to a creaky old network which grounded thousands of planes in the US over the weekend.

Problems with the NSA's internal communication system and, separately, Apple's iCloud and Apple Pay were also blamed on things going wrong during system updates.

Industry observers note that disruption to payments in US banks has been put down to a "software bug" from JP Morgan Chase. The bank says it is not a cyber-security incident.

But some recent problems are in fact related to cyber-security vulnerabilities.

AI was used to discover flaws in Java, the third-most popular programming language in the world.

And researcher Scott Helme also discovered malicious code in Optimizely, a platform which is used by some of the world's largest companies.

Hackers were able to steal about $7,000 (Â£5,300) in cryptocurrency before they were discovered and kicked out.

Let's address the cyber incidents first - and the role of newly introduced AI models.

"I would be very surprised if all of these cyber-attacks and the attackers behind them were not using AI to accelerate themselves," Helme told BBC Radio 4's Today programme.

Since the summer, AI companies such as Anthropic and OpenAI have decided it's too risky to give the general public access to the most advanced models before allowing certain companies and institutions to use them.

In these cases, security professionals can use the same tech to find flaws themselves and alert others before criminals get to them.

This happened in the recent Java incident, as well as the PHP one - where there was a scramble to patch the flaws before they could be exploited.

These are not new - the world stood still in recent years as CrowdStrike and then Amazon Web Services ground many devices and systems to a halt.

Part of this may be down to technical debt - a term used when operators go for quick fixes to software instead of solving an underlying problem.

A build-up of surface-level fixes can eventually end up with more serious problems down the line.

With AI causing many companies to re-evaluate their cyber-security, they may be issuing more updates to patch flaws they have found.

Outages are often also a reminder of the physical tech our online world relies on - such as cables, servers and data centres.

In the US air traffic control incident, for example, the problem came as engineers were replacing outdated copper wires with fibre.

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio is adamant the recent incidents are not connected or coming from one attacker.

"We are not under systemic attack," he said. "These events, one has nothing to do with the other, they are not inter-related.".

In cases such as PHP and Java, the "good guys" found the flaws, with the help of AI, before the bad guys - allowing them to get together some fixes before the bugs could be widely exploited.

At the same time, Anthropic, among other leading AI firms, has warned of the risks its own products pose.

Outages in particular have an impact on the physical world as they often cause disruption to healthcare, schools and people's ability to do their jobs.

Or, in the case over the weekend, planes' ability to fly.

They also shine a light on the world's heavy reliance on a small number of players.

Not just frontier AI companies, but infrastructure providers such as Microsoft and Amazon, which - along with Google - run a huge chunk of the world's computer servers.

In the PHP incident, the world was holding its breath as one programmer - working voluntarily - worked to problem patches in a form that was easily applicable.

And Java, run by another tech giant Oracle, is used by nearly all of the biggest companies on Earth.

"The Oracle Java vulnerability is a stark reminder of how dependent businesses have become on a relatively small number of critical technologies," said Sheila Pancholi, risk assurance lead at consultancy firm RSM UK.

"Organisations need to know where their critical technology dependencies sit, act rapidly when vulnerabilities emerge, and test that their response has actually worked.".

Meanwhile Xander Van Raemdonck, security engineer at Aikido Security, said the challenge was "knowing whether you're exposed".

"Java is bundled inside a huge amount of business software, so many companies don't have a full list of where it runs," he remarked.

The AI tools used to help find security flaws are still so new that they will probably continue to find vulnerabilities which have been lingering in code for decades.

"AI is accelerating discovery on both sides and speeding up how much software gets shipped," noted Martin Reynolds, field CTO at software delivery manufacturer Harness.

"So the volume of what developers need to patch is going up, while the time they have to get the work done it is going down.".

Tech debt will need to continue to be dealt with - which means more upgrades to systems and more potential for these to cause disruptions.

Plus there is the constant threat from hackers as they continue to get more sophisticated.

Attackers only need to find one vulnerability to exploit - whereas defenders have to protect against all potential hacks.

"Attackers can move rapidly and carefree, and experiment without risk of failure so they can naturally often move a fraction faster," Helme remarked.

"We're presently observing that small delay between how fast the attackers can move and how fast the defenders can move.".

The cat-and-mouse nature of the cyber-security game means it's unlikely to end any time soon - and the impact of AI means the stakes are getting higher.
