Barely a week after the release of GTA 6, efforts are already under way to get an unofficial version playable on PC, although some projects have faced takedown notices from the game's publisher.

For some gamers, it's an exciting prospect, despite the legal questions surrounding the process.

For developers, it may be a warning: if a game as big as GTA 6 can be recreated, what does that mean for everyone else?

The recent wave of AI-assisted game recreation has been widely linked to Anthropic's Opus 5.5 model, released in September, and to agentic tools such as Claude Code.

Mark Brown, creator of the YouTube channel Game Maker's Toolkit and whose own game Word Play was recently recreated using AI, said cloning largely took two forms: replication and reverse engineering.

Replication is where someone asks an AI chatbot such as Claude or ChatGPT to build a upcoming game based on an existing one, possibly using screenshots or a description.

This, he said, was similar to "looking at a painting and making your own replica" â something which is "a very close copy, but is ultimately a latest painting".

It's a particular issue among smaller studios, many of whom worry that AI makes it easier for others to copy their work.

When indie developers Sodoj and Marco from the studio Robotonic noticed a version of their unreleased puzzle game 204Snake appear on the free games platform Yandex with the same name, they were "appalled".

"The game was essentially the exact same idea, the exact same mechanics as we had, but with the art looking a bit off," Marco told the BBC.

The second approach â reverse engineering â involves working backwards from a finished game to understand how it was created.

One way of doing this is decompilation, which attempts to turn the game's final version into something closer to its original source code.

Brown compares the process to "taking a cake and somehow turning it back into the original recipe, with the exact quantities of flour, eggs, and milk used, so anyone can remake the same cake".

Reverse-engineering projects have helped bring games such as Halo: Combat Evolved and Grand Theft Auto: Vice City to web browsers.

A related technique, recompilation, can help adapt a game that has been reverse-engineered to run on different hardware. This is one of the methods being explored in efforts to get GTA 6 onto PC.

Coders wanting to attempt this with Rockstar's latest game face a difficult task because of its sheer size and complexity.
