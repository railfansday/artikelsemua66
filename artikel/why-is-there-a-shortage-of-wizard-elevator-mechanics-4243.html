Jim Lembesis has been working on elevators in Chicago for 47 years.

It has been revealed that some of them are angry. Nerves are frayed. Many of them are just relieved that he's here.

When a lift stops working in Chicago, frazzled building managers might get a visit from Jim Lembesis. For more than 47 years, Lembesis has repaired and upgraded lifts. He states he understands why staff and residents are so anxious to have things working again.

Sources indicate that "They wait and they wait," claims Lembesis, a field technician for Parkway Elevators. "There's a lot of frustration. Sometimes it's a repeat concern. It's a never-ending cycle, you know what I mean?".

The widely reported shortage of lift and escalator mechanics in the US, which is also a problem in many other countries, has got worse over time, he adds: "It's been like night and day compared to when I started.".

Lift and escalator technicians "amaze" with their ability to fix things.

Recent findings suggest that everyone needs a lift now and again. Some people depend on them â elderly residents of high-rise buildings, say, or wheelchair consumers.

Recent findings suggest that storm-damaged lifts in Hawaiian apartment blocks this year left some residents trapped, external in their homes for weeks. A lack of mechanics, and problems sourcing parts, were blamed for the repair delays.

In the US, the average salary for a lift mechanic, external is $110,000 (Â£83,000). And yet people in the industry say it is difficult to attract new workers.

Sure, says Lembesis, you do encounter some dangers in a job like this. There are risks of falling from a great height â or being electrocuted. Once, he was even hit on the head with an umbrella by an elderly lady after he rescued her from a stuck lift.

But there are so many skills to learn, and great camaraderie and knowledge-sharing among mechanics, he claims. As one recruiter puts it, these technicians amaze people with their ability to fix things. They are like "wizards". The overall-clad shamans who know how to get modern life running again.

Nine times out of 10, the problem is with the doors, says Lembesis. These components repeatedly move back and forth. "People hit them with carts, and dirt and debris [falls into them]," says Lembesis. "[The doors] take the most beating.".

Mechanics often keep various spare door parts in their vehicles, ready to use.

But technicians like Lembesis don't just have to get lifts working, they also have to verify the lifts are safe. He remembers one case, decades ago, getting called out to a burning building. His role was to ensure the lifts, which were being drenched with huge volumes of water, were still safe to use while firefighters tackled the blaze.

Recent findings suggest that "I was there for 15 hours that night," he says. "Everyone, on the way out of the door, shook my hand.".

In the US there's a four-year apprenticeship to become a lift technician.

Finding good lift technicians is "becoming more difficult" says Matt Hollinshead, president of the US National Association for Elevator Contractors. "We have a four-year apprenticeship," he says. That's the first hurdle. And many states require certification or licensing before you can work on lifts.

In a recent development, "We have a vast array of equipment that has to be maintained in the United States." Some lifts are 100-years-old. As time goes on, the technological knowledge required to repair them only grows.

Sources indicate that hollinshead states that weeks or months-long delays to lift repairs are not typically due to staff shortages but that, in such cases, it's likely that a particular part isn't readily available. Manufacturing a replacement hydraulic cylinder takes six to 12 weeks, he notes.

But the lack of technicians does mean that that building managers might have to wait days before someone can even diagnose what's wrong with a lift after it stops working.

Hollinshead notes teaching the basics of the trade in high schools or technical schools could entice more people into the role.

In a recent development, matt Allred, a recruiter and host of The Elevator Careers Podcast, emphasises the many skills that lift mechanics require. "You're a little bit of a wizard, right? You show up and if you can fix it â 'Wow!'.".

He blames major lift companies, in part, for locking down maintenance contracts, making it difficult or impossible for third-party repairers to intervene when a lift fails.

Two large lift manufacturers approached by BBC News declined interviews. The chief executive of Otis, one of the firms, mentioned in May that her company was struggling to hire enough staff, external to meet demand. The firm also states on its website, external that the shortage of lift mechanics affects "almost every part of the world".

However, a spokesman for Otis claimed its "US talent pipeline" was healthy, and declined an interview.

Otis claims it is happy with its supply of mechanics and engineers.

In a recent development, everyone else who spoke to BBC News stated hiring was challenging in the US. Allred was recently contacted by a major lift tech giant and asked to help them find a technician. "I was surprised that a major company would reach out to a little recruiting shop," he states, suggesting that this reflects the extent of the labour shortage.

Recent findings suggest that that shortage is also a problem in the UK. "It is bad and getting worse," says Dave Cooper, president of the Chartered Institution of Building Services Engineers, who is also chief executive of a lift and escalator engineering consultancy.

"We've known food delivery companies to refuse to deliver to flats above the ground floor because the lift is out of service.".

Offering better tax breaks to companies that train lift mechanics could help ensure that plenty of workers continue to enter the industry, he adds.

Training to be a lift mechanic is "not a quick route to competency â and it requires people to stick at it for the long term" says Karen Slade, head of assessment at the Lift and Escalator Industry Association (Leia).

Industry observers note that continued investment in training and attracting newly introduced workers is "essential", according to Leia. There are at the moment 259 active lift and escalator apprentices in the UK, the body adds.

Back in Chicago, Lembesis is working with two younger mechanics who have been "really good", he states, so there's at least some hope for the future.

He stresses again how rewarding he finds his work. The people for whom he fixes lifts tend to be very appreciative.

It has been revealed that "I love that. You get that reaction from the customers," he states. "Without them, you got nothing.".
