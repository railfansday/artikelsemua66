Magnific passes $300M ARR and may raise outside money next year

🌐 Source: Thenextweb • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (926 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Magnific, the Spanish AI creative platform once called Freepik, has not raised a funding round since 2010.
  • “On outside investment, we have no looks today, but nothing is set in stone.
  • Magnific stated on Tuesday that it has passed $300M in annual recurring revenue.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Joaquín Cuenca, co-founder and CEO of Magnific.

Magnific, the Spanish AI creative platform once called Freepik, has not raised a funding round since 2010. Its customers have paid for everything, including the move into AI, co-founder and chief executive Joaquín Cuenca told TNW.

“On outside investment, we have no looks today, but nothing is set in stone. The investment this market requires is very significant, and if taking capital lets us accelerate, it may happen, maybe next year,” Cuenca mentioned.

Magnific stated on Tuesday that it has passed $300M in annual recurring revenue. It had $230M when it rebranded from Freepik in April. It is opening offices in Shanghai and Singapore and will spend $10M in Asia Pacific through early 2027.

It has been revealed that magnific says that makes it Europe’s fourth-largest generative AI company by ARR, on publicly reported figures. Only Lovable, ElevenLabs and Mistral rank higher. Mistral raised €3bn in September, and ElevenLabs doubled its valuation to $22bn in a share sale.

It has been revealed that magnific is still bootstrapped, Cuenca said. EQT took a majority stake in 2020. It bought shares from his partners and never put new money into the firm.

Magnific is EBITDA positive, he remarked. Profit is thinner than revenue suggests, because most of the money goes straight back into AI compute. Every time a better image or video model comes out, people want it that same day, he added.

“We already spend hundreds of millions of dollars generating what our consumers ask for,” Cuenca remarked.

An IPO is not on the table today, Cuenca remarked. Public markets now ask for far bigger companies than they used to. His target is to pass $1bn in revenue by the end of next year. He expects to get there organically.

“What I value is that there is no timer on us,” Cuenca noted.

According to recent reports, several of the best video models are Chinese, and users pick them because they win on quality, Cuenca said. Magnific runs ByteDance’s Seedance, Kuaishou’s Kling, Alibaba’s Wan and MiniMax’s models today.

In a recent development, until last year he had never been to China, and Magnific’s suppliers were American. He has been several times since, once for just 20 hours. China entered Magnific’s top 10 markets this year for the first time.

Magnific is not trying to build its own foundation model to take on Google, ByteDance or OpenAI, Cuenca said. For an independent company, he called that “a lost battle”.

“Think of a MacBook: the models are the CPU, and we build the rest of the machine, the tools, the workflows, the controls,” Cuenca mentioned.

The Shanghai office puts Magnific next to the teams that build those models, he remarked, so it can add new ones faster and pass on feedback from its customers. According to the release, Singapore will be its sales base in the region, alongside existing teams in South Korea, Japan and Thailand.

“Studios need legal guarantees before they use anything in production, and meeting that bar is one of the reasons we are growing with them,” Cuenca mentioned.

Recent findings suggest that magnific’s Enterprise prepares include indemnification against third-party IP claims on what the platform produces, Cuenca said. Customers own what they create, and Magnific does not use their inputs or outputs to train models, he said.

“The exception is bad faith: if you ask for Mickey Mouse and you get Mickey Mouse, that’s on you,” he noted.

According to recent reports, in his view, responsibility has to move to the output. AI can infringe without the user meaning to, so what matters is detecting it and flagging it to the user.

“With any tool you can infringe: if you can draw two overlapping circles, you can infringe Mastercard’s trademark,” he mentioned.

Sources indicate that in Singapore, Hagia Labs made the films for telco StarHub’s Unlimited+ Looks campaign, with its creative partner Superson. StarHub’s green characters, the Hublings, fly across Asia and land in Japan. Hagia built the characters and settings in Magnific’s Spaces tool. Done the conventional way, Cuenca remarked, that would have meant travel, crews and a shoot in every location.

According to recent reports, by Hagia’s own estimate, it took two to four weeks from brief to delivery. A conventional shoot would take two to four months.

Recent findings suggest that teams start by doing the same work faster, Cuenca stated. Then they make what they could not afford before. Brands that used to advertise with a still image and a line of copy now make film-quality spots, and studios are greenlighting fantasy and historical projects that used to die on budget.

Recent findings suggest that the next step is automation, he said. A team builds a workflow once in Spaces, and staff or their agents in ChatGPT or Claude can run it through Magnific’s MCP server.

Enterprise adoption in the region grew 35%, which is why Magnific is hiring there instead of flying people in from Málaga, Cuenca noted. The release indicates Magnific looks more than 20 staff in Asia Pacific by the end of 2026, and twice that in 2027. Product and engineering stay at its Málaga headquarters. Cuenca called the offices “a long-term commitment to Asia” in Magnific’s release.

“Being funded by customers forces a discipline I don’t want to lose: you only build what people will pay for,” Cuenca mentioned.
