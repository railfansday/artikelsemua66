Not Safe, Not Creative: 123456 Is Still the World’s Most Common Password

Our data keeps getting hacked, breached, stolen and exposed, and yet we’re still using the most predictable passwords.

It has been revealed that for the seventh time in the past eight years, 123456 is the world’s most common password, and it’s got other uncreative companions on the list, according to the latest analysis by password and passkey manager NordPass, a division of Nord Security. The top three most used passwords in the US were: 123456, password and admin. Yikes.

According to recent reports, in the 44 countries analyzed, the password 123456 was used more than 7.2 million times. The rest of the top 10 passwords: admin, 12345678, 123456789, 1234, 12345, password, Aa123456, Pass@123 and 1234567890.

Sources indicate that nordPass said that even though many people prefer simple, easy-to-remember passwords, much more awareness of password safety is needed. The firm recommends creating a unique password for each account that’s at least 15 characters long, including uppercase and lowercase letters, numbers and special characters. Though it’s tempting to reuse the same passwords, hackers rely on that complacency.

NordPass suggests upgrading weak, old passwords to stronger ones. You can also add multifactor authentication, which requires an additional layer of security beyond a login password. That could mean entering a one-time passcode sent by text or email, answering security questions, or using facial recognition or fingerprint ID.

CNET managing editor Adam Benjamin noted that the risk isn’t primarily from someone randomly guessing your password. The danger is from algorithms that collect data or bots that use credential stuffing (in other words, stolen username and password pairs obtained from a data breach).

According to Benjamin, using an obscure reference or a different language doesn’t make a password stronger. “A password is good when it’s too much trouble for an algorithm to crack,” he said.

You might remember the scene from the 1987 movie Spaceballs, where the combination to Planet Druidia’s air shield is 12345. Dark Helmet calls it “the stupidest combination I’ve ever heard in my life!” and states it’s “the kind of thing an idiot would have on his luggage.” The joke remains a staple reference for weak passwords and PINs, and yet, here we are.

Last year, two researchers hacked into McDonald’s hiring chatbot by guessing the password 123456 to access 64 million data records, including applicant names, email addresses and phone numbers.

Recent findings suggest that in 2023, the Akira ransomware group hacked into KNP Logistics Group, a 158‑year‑old UK transportation firm, by guessing an employee’s weak password. The hackers locked the firm’s data and demanded a $6.6 million ransom, forcing KNP to shut down and lay off 700 workers.

This year, suspected Iranian hackers gained access to US water supply systems due to internet-connected devices that used default passwords — or none at all.

The rise of the AI era has also made password hacking easier. One study even found that some AI systems could determine people’s passwords by listening to their keystrokes.

NordPass has conducted its Top 200 Most Common Passwords analysis since 2019. To compile its lists, NordPass and independent researchers examined passwords exposed in public data breaches and dark web repositories, then organized and categorized the data by country of origin.

NordPass has found that password habits are heavily influenced by current trends and culture. Over the last several years, some of the top three passwords have been Lebron0319 (in reference to NBA star LeBron James), Savage20 (musical group) and roblox.com1 (video game). In the 2010s, the top three were Lachimolala (meme), telegram (social media) and minecraft (video game).

Sources indicate that but while these passwords might make us feel nostalgic for these time capsules of experiences, interests and entertainment, they don’t translate to strong internet security. “Unfortunately, fandoms make for terrible passwords,” the NordPass report notes.

Sources indicate that passwords based on pop culture, current events and sports can be easily predicted or cracked in seconds using automated tools, especially if your interests are visible on public social media profiles. Adding a number to the end of a password doesn’t help — attackers program their tools to test common number combinations and symbol swaps.

Given all the accounts we deal with — financial institutions, social media, stores, government agencies — creating dozens of unique passwords and remembering them can be exhausting and annoying.

“Inventing passwords that meet that criterion across hundreds of logins is virtually impossible, which is why we recommend password managers to create long, complex passwords and then store them all for you,” mentioned Benjamin.

Industry observers note that A password manager creates and updates passwords for apps, Wi-Fi networks and websites, ensuring that all your passwords are unique and resistant to bot attacks. Another plus is that you don’t have to remember them or write them down: They’re stored in an encrypted vault you can access by verifying your identity. Password managers can also auto-fill login credentials.

CNET senior writer Joe Supan remarked the best password managers also offer passkeys, which are ways of logging into apps and websites using biometric ID, such as facial or fingerprint scans, or PIN codes. “These are really the sign-in methods of the future, and I probably wouldn’t recommend a password manager that doesn’t include them at this point,” Supan said.

CNET’s top two recommendations for password managers are NordPass and Bitwarden.
