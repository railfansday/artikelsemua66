📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Smaller size can mean greater effort.

Recent findings suggest that building a custom PC is a wonderfully satisfying feeling. Even if you've never built one for yourself, it's ingrained itself into the world of content creation and social media so fiercely that everyone has most likely at least watched someone partially build a PC at some point.

According to recent reports, as you may have noticed, there are so many different form factors for cases and unique enclosures. One of the more interesting ones that has been carving out its own funky niche in the market is the mini PC build. Because of the unique form factors and limited space these cases allow, they come with challenges that make a build of this type a bit more difficult than normal.

Unlike larger mid- and full-range towers, the mini PC builds tend to focus on a straight-ahead approach with very little wiggle room for frills. Things that we take for granted in larger builds, like room for maximum airflow or a larger GPU, become pertinent struggles in the mini PC world.

One of the core things to understand upfront about mini PC builds is the case itself. What we know as mini PCs tend to be built within Mini-ITX or microATX cases. For as small as they are, you might think they'd cost significantly less than their larger siblings. In a lot of situations, that's true, but Mini-ITX cases also come in a lot of different types. Some of them offer fun bells and whistles, which can run your bill up before you know it.

A lot of the mainstream mini computers that you can buy from companies, like Apple's Mac mini or certain models of the ASUS NUC, are built with an SoC — a system-on-a-chip that combines a CPU with a GPU — and RAM sometimes soldered into the motherboard itself. Doing something like that isn't really feasible, but you can get fairly close with some commercial cases.

Recent findings suggest that no matter which one you're looking at, it may be reassuring to know that despite their small stature, a lot of them are surprisingly roomy. You're still going to be limited by what the case allows in terms of dimensions and compatibility, but those are all easy to contrast and compare with the official specs or using a site like PCPartPicker to determine if a component will fit in your case.

According to recent reports, the unorthodox shape and size of a lot of mini PC cases means that while it's entirely possible to fill them with a lot of cool stuff, your approach is going to have to be a bit more economical and predetermined. For most of your situations, a mini ITX case will only accommodate a mini-ITX motherboard, while microATX cases will be able to fit in both Mini-ITX and microATX motherboards due to the micro cases being a bit bigger on average.

Recent findings suggest that airflow also becomes something of a problem, as the smaller size of these cases means your internal components have a lot less room to breathe. There's usually very little space once all the guts are mashed inside, and that's especially true if you go for a discrete GPU as opposed to an APU that combines the CPU and the GPU. The prices of most small form factor, or SFF, PC components are also a bit steep.

SFF power supplies, motherboards, CPU coolers and GPUs can all contribute a surprising amount to the final price of a machine, which may make going for a standard ATX build more appealing. Finding all these unique-sized parts isn't even the most challenging glitch when building a mini PC, as the physical build itself can be far more difficult than that of a standard ATX machine.

There's a lot less room, but you also need to be more careful with the actual installation of parts. A standard ATX build can largely be put together in any order, but mini PC builds need to be put together in a specific order due to the part sizes and clearance concerns.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Better first layers, fewer failed prints and a few calibration tests worth knowing about.

Your 3D printer can spend hours making a part, only for it to detach from the bed halfway through. Worse, the finished piece might look great but refuse to fit where it should. That's a lot of filament down the drain, all because the printer might need calibration.

According to recent reports, an uneven bed, incorrect nozzle height and poorly tuned extrusion can cause adhesion issues, rough surfaces and dimensional inaccuracies in an FDM 3D printer. Calibration can address those problems on both older printers that need manual adjustments and newer models that handle some checks automatically. It's also worth checking calibration after moving your printer, adjusting belts, replacing a nozzle or performing mechanical maintenance.

The calibration process starts with a mechanical check, cleaning, bed leveling, Z-offset adjustments (when applicable) and a first-layer test. E-step calibration (rotation-distance calibration in Klipper) with a heated or printing-temperature hotend is a good indicator on supported printers to see if the extruder is extruding the right amount of filament before flow rate tuning. The temperature towers can then be used to fine-tune filament settings. There is no fixed schedule for recalibration, so repeat the appropriate checks as you change hardware or if print quality begins to deteriorate.

In a recent development, start with the hardware. Set the printer on a stable surface, and if that's in a bedroom or home office, check whether the space is suitable for 3D printing. With the machine powered off and cooled, inspect for loose belts or toolhead components, then clean the build plate according to the manufacturer's instructions. If your desk is wobbly, the Calibrate button won't fix anything.

Then follow your printer's bed-leveling procedure, often labeled Calibration, Leveling or Self-check. Many newer machines from Bambu Lab, Creality, Prusa and Anycubic can measure the bed automatically, something worth considering if you're buying a 3D printer anytime soon. Some models, including the Bambu Lab A1 and Prusa MK4S, can also set the first-layer height automatically. The routines differ by model, so follow your printer's instructions.

Sources indicate that by contrast, certain machines, such as the Elegoo Neptune 4 and some Creality Ender models, have manual adjustment wheels, so they need a little more attention. Follow the manufacturer's leveling instructions, adjusting the wheels until a sheet of paper slides between the nozzle and bed with slight resistance at the specified points. Then run automatic leveling afterward if your printer supports it.

Finally, print a single-layer square. The lines should touch and appear slightly flattened. Visible gaps can indicate that the nozzle is too high, while raised ridges or scraping suggest it's too close. If your printer allows manual Z-offset adjustments, make small changes and repeat the test.

For E-step calibration, measure the filament, set a measured feed and compare the actual feed, making adjustments as necessary. Klipper has an equivalent setting called rotation distance, so before optimizing filament flow, it makes sense to check this.

In a recent development, it's not enough to get the first layer correct. Even filaments made from the same material can behave differently. One temperature or flow setting may be perfect for one spool but cause problems for another. Be sure to check the calibration options in your slicer.

Recent findings suggest that bambu Studio includes Flow Dynamics and Flow Rate tests, while Creality Print and OrcaSlicer have their own calibration tools. PrusaSlicer also lets you adjust the extrusion multiplier. You don't need to switch slicers to run a test print.

It has been revealed that A temperature tower test prints sections at different nozzle temperatures, letting you compare stringing, bridging and surface quality. Some slicers generate these tests automatically, while others require a downloaded model with temperature changes configured manually. Make sure those temperature changes are configured. Otherwise, you've just printed a tower.

Recent findings suggest that flow-rate calibration is done by testing patches for over-extrusion (extruding too much filament, resulting in buildup) or under-extrusion (extruding too little, resulting in gaps). You can also print a 3DBenchy to examine overhangs and details, or a calibration cube to check basic dimensional consistency. According to NASA, the Moon goes through a complete cycle of phases roughly every 29.5 days. The cycle begins with a Latest Moon, progresses to a Full Moon, and then gradually returns to a New Moon before starting all over again.

Fresh Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

First Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere).

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.

And you won't find more impressive spec sheets than Samsung OLEDs. Since its return to the OLED TV market in 2022, the company has invested heavily in display-panel innovations to carve out its own niche. Based on that, you might think Samsung would be the clear winner for OLED TVs, yet LG's latest OLED innovations make the decision less black-and-white.

Industry observers note that samsung has continued to struggle to provide accurate information about its televisions, obfuscating which panel technologies it uses. LG has faced similar criticism for its major quality concerns with the launch of the G6. Finding the perfect television goes far beyond finding a good panel type. The brand, the television's software and your viewing area should play a key role in your decision.

OLED televisions come in three display panel types: WOLED, QD-OLED, and RGB Tandem OLED. WOLED is the most common display type that LG pioneered and continues to innovate on. These TVs use a white light source that passes through four filters to create color: red, green, blue, and white. The white subpixel layer helps brighten the image after it passes through the filters, but it comes at the cost of text clarity. This is the main technology you'll find in LG televisions and is ideal for high ambient-light environments.

Sources indicate that samsung shook up the OLED space in 2022 with QD-OLED. This technology uses a blue light source that passes through red and green quantum dots. Since the dots convert color rather than filtering it out, these panels tend to have a much wider color gamut and are significantly more energy efficient, which theoretically means they last longer. They're the gold standard for HDR displays, but quantum dot technology makes them ill-suited for bright living spaces; strong light hitting the panel can cause magenta glare.

Industry observers note that think of iCloud Private Relay as two separate layers that protect your web browsing. Apple runs the first layer and sees your original IP address, but not the website you're visiting.
