A major scandal involving Nvidia founder Jensen Huang and a suspected Chinese spy based in Singapore has cast a spotlight on the city-state's success as a global chip hub - and a location for alleged illicit trade.

It has been revealed that on Thursday, Huang stepped down from the US chip giant after admitting he had an affair. He was reportedly involved with a woman named Alice Huang, who ran Singaporean data centre tech giant Megaspeed, a major buyer of Nvidia's chips.

For decades, Singapore has painstakingly cultivated a reputation as an open, business-friendly economy offering easy access to both the US and Chinese markets.

With the AI boom, it has become a major hub for the trade of high-end semiconductor chips in recent years.

Singapore was the second-largest billing centre globally last year for Nvidia, accounting for nearly a fifth of what the US chip giant earns.

But notably, less than 1% of its earnings were for chips that actually entered Singapore, as the island acts as a hub for many Asian companies to place orders and send them elsewhere.

Since 2022, when the US restricted advanced American semiconductors from being exported to China or acquired by Chinese companies, US officials have been concerned that other countries such as Singapore could be used by Chinese companies to gain access to the chips.

It became the biggest buyer of Nvidia's chips in South East Asia and is suspected of giving backdoor access to these chips to Chinese companies via its data centres in Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand.

While Singapore does not prohibit the transfer of advanced AI chips to companies abroad, such moves can breach US export controls.

Singapore has remarked that while it does not enforce "unilateral export controls imposed by other countries" it expects all companies in its jurisdiction to respect such regulations.

The city-state has had some success in policing this.

It has charged four companies and five individuals - including Chinese nationals - for various offences including fraud, following US concerns that Chinese AI company DeepSeek had been buying Nvidia chips from third parties in Singapore, among other places.

They also tried to probe Megaspeed, but were faced with a number of difficulties.

Megaspeed's office in Singapore.

In a statement released on Thursday, the government said that since February 2025, they had been looking into whether Megaspeed and other firms had made false declarations about data servers which were purchased and exported through Singapore.

The companies stated the equipment would end up in neighbouring Malaysia, but Singapore authorities suspect the equipment's final destinations were elsewhere.

Singapore said it sought assistance from US authorities several times to get more information from one of the investigated companies - Supermicro, a US-based firm which the BBC understands was supplying computer servers to Megaspeed.

In a recent development, but "despite these repeated requests, Singapore has to date received limited information or assistance", it said in its statement. The BBC has sought comment from the US State Department.

Singapore authorities said they had also investigated Alice Huang - not for espionage, but for any possible role in Megaspeed's suspected fraud.

In the end, they found "insufficient evidence" of crime and let her leave Singapore in February this year, after holding her passport for several months.

Since launching their probe nearly two years ago, Singapore has yet to bring any charges against Megaspeed or any of the companies and individuals linked to the case.

Singapore and US investigations into Megaspeed are still ongoing.

In 2024, US officials had paid a visit to a Malaysian data centre owned by Megaspeed and saw Nvidia technology still in their boxes, raising concerns that Megaspeed was shipping high-end chips out elsewhere, according to a report by the Newly introduced York Times.

Last year, Megaspeed appeared to have imported 50,000 more chips than Nvidia could account for, according to a Bloomberg report. Nvidia later adjusted its own figures after visiting more warehouses.

Megaspeed has maintained it has always acted lawfully and that previous inspections by authorities found no evidence of illegal activity.

On Thursday, the organization mentioned it "welcomes appropriate government scrutiny" and that it is "committed to working constructively and transparently with the US government".

China has also long rebuffed accusations that its companies and institutions are illegally obtaining high-end US chips.

Jensen Huang resigned from Nvidia on Thursday following a board meeting.

How Megaspeed and Alice Huang came to Singapore highlights other vulnerabilities stemming from the city-state's openness.

In recent years, the city-state has become a prime destination for Chinese companies, with tech firms such as TikTok and Manus making it their global headquarters. Besides Singapore's ease of doing business, it has a population that is mostly ethnically Chinese and largely views China positively.

For a Chinese firm, Singapore offers a base near the mainland and a "neutral address" that global customers and investors accept, said Winston Ma, adjunct professor at Latest York University's School of Law.

But critics say this has given rise to "Singapore-washing", where Chinese companies use the city-state's clean image to obscure their origins, reduce suspicions and even cover up dubious activities.

Washington-based security analysis group C4ADS told the BBC they had assessed Alice Huang's setup of Megaspeed to be such a case.

BBC checks of corporate records in China, Hong Kong and Singapore found that in 2019, Huang sold a gaming firm and its subsidiaries to Chinese gaming firm 7Road.

It has been revealed that in March 2023, a 7Road subsidiary in Singapore named Huang as its executive director. The next day it changed its name - to Megaspeed.

Huang was in charge for eight months before she transferred the company to a Singaporean man.

During this period, Megaspeed went through "a critical period of corporate restructuring and regional expansion", and her corporate relationships raised questions about whether "Megaspeed's Singaporean identity is genuine or effectively Chinese-controlled", according to C4ADS.

Even after she transferred Megaspeed, C4ADS assessed that she was still "practically in control" of the company, based on her links to Megaspeed via another company as well as LinkedIn posts where she continued to identify herself as Megaspeed's "chairwoman" at conferences.

Megaspeed has not answered BBC queries regarding the tech giant's and Alice Huang's links to China and the Chinese government.

Alice Huang, seen here in Taipei with Jensen Huang last year, ran Megaspeed for several months in 2023.

All this reinforces the dilemma faced by Singapore.

"For the longest time, Singapore has managed to enjoy the best of both worlds with excellent relations with US and China," Nanyang Technological University politics and international studies lecturer Dylan Loh stated.

Recent findings suggest that singapore has long positioned itself as a neutral party between the two superpowers. Its prime minister Lawrence Wong has insisted that his country is neither pro-China nor pro-America, but "pro-Singapore".

But Loh notes this balancing act "has now fast become very difficult to manage and sometimes quite fraught too" with the US-China dispute over chips.

With the Megaspeed case, the tightrope Singapore has walked is set to get even narrower, according to analysts.

Beijing may ask why Singapore is investigating complications pertaining to US restrictions and whether they are siding with the US' interests, says Chong Ja Ian, a Singaporean political scientist and non-resident scholar with Carnegie China.

Washington, in turn, might read Singapore's latest statement as "pushing blame" on the US, which could lead to backlash from the White House, he said.

In a recent development, "Singapore has made a policy position not to take sides. But by not choosing a side, they run the risk of being seen as complicit" in either side's agenda, he said.

But Benjamin Ho, an assistant professor and China expert at Singapore's S Rajaratnam School of International Studies, noted that this was "not the first or last time Singapore will be caught between the US and China".

"This is the price that an open economy has to grapple with - you get caught in the middle when things turn sour.".

According to recent reports, why so many? Easy: in Linux, any bug can be a security bug. As Linux Torvalds told me in 2024, “Bugs will happen, and anything can be a security bug if somebody is clever enough to just figure out how to abuse it.” Besides, since Linux runs everything from smartphones to refrigerators to supercomputers, what may be a serious bug in one device may not even appear at all on another.

In a recent development, it’s not just Linux, though. In the latest Windows Patch Tuesday security release, Microsoft reported 726 CVEs. The vast majority of these bug reports are coming from AI programmers. Finding bugs on anything is no longer the hard part. But reviewing and fixing them is, and both Microsoft developers and kernel maintainers are struggling to keep up.

As the FORGE Lab noted, “Finding bugs and fixing them are drastically out of balance.” Turning its findings into reports took more than four person-months because “human effort [is] the bottleneck.”.

It has been revealed that that’s hurting the overworked maintainers. On Nov. 10, an ARM engineer and Linux kernel maintainer Lorenzo Stoakes gave up reviewing code. He’d been a reviewer for the memory-management core, misc, reclaim, and Rust-related sections. Stoakes, a kernel contributor since 2014,  wrote, “Given the current throughput in the kernel resulting from AI-generated series, I unfortunately lack the bandwidth to generally be able to offer review except in those areas I maintain.”.

It has been revealed that stoakes added, “Of course the AI wave has been horrible, and is getting significantly worse. At first it was once in a while that I’d see slop. Then it was at least once a week. Then every few days. Nowadays it’s pretty much half of what I see, if not more.”.

It’s Getting Too Dry for Even Cactuses to Survive

📅 15 Nov 2026 | ⏱️ 5 min read (963 words, 📚 Deep Dive) | Source: WIRED

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Recent findings suggest that yet cacti are in serious trouble, due to habitat loss and the poaching of species for private collections.
  • “The public perception of cacti tends to be of these organisms that are extremely drought-adapted and are just resilient, can handle anything that you throw at them,” said Michiel Pillet, a conservation ecologist at the University of South Florida and lead author of the paper.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The humble cactus is an icon of resiliency. Stuck in a desert with little rain and abundant searing sun, these plants don’t just survive, they thrive—and in turn provide habitat for owls and other birds, and flowers for bats and other pollinators.

Recent findings suggest that yet cacti are in serious trouble, due to habitat loss and the poaching of species for private collections. Now we can add worsening droughts to those woes, according to a upcoming study in the journal Nature. Researchers modeled a future of not just relentlessly rising temperatures but also the attendant dry spells, finding that the majority of nearly 1,000 cactus species will lose habitat as the climate radically changes. More than a quarter of those species could lose at least 80 percent of that habitat by 2100.

“The public perception of cacti tends to be of these organisms that are extremely drought-adapted and are just resilient, can handle anything that you throw at them,” said Michiel Pillet, a conservation ecologist at the University of South Florida and lead author of the paper. “In reality, cacti are absolutely amazing—and they’re my favorite plant group, clearly—but they’re not that different from other plants.”.

It has been revealed that that means cacti need water. In the deserts of the southwestern United States, for example, they rely on seasonal monsoon rains, which tend to make July or August the wettest month in a given year. Because this is still a desert, cacti have evolved a range of adaptations to survive, like a waxy skin that helps them retain that bounty of rainwater. “Just like other plants, they evolved under conditions that they’re used to,” Pillet stated. “When you take them outside of those conditions, they might not be able to handle that.”.

Think of these cacti like sick hospital patients. Someone might be admitted for heat-related illness, as their body struggles to cool itself. At the same time, though, they might be dehydrated. Cacti are dealing with the same stresses, as global temperatures rise and dry spells intensify. “When we consider drought conditions, then it turns out that they’re much, much sicker than we previously thought,” Pillet stated. “These results really do change the outlook of cacti, and most likely plants more broadly under climate change.”.

In a recent development, instead of simply modeling these threats going forward, the researchers turned to the citizen science platform iNaturalist, where people upload photos of plants and animals. This provided 650,000 observations of cacti in the wild, helping paint a clearer picture of how species might be faring.

Really, this isn’t a simple matter of cacti withering and dying. Sure, if relentless droughts take hold, individuals can’t get water. But the stress could be temporally complex, too. “Now we can move towards questions of: What life stages are most affected?” Pillet remarked. “Is it the adults or is it the seedlings? But also, which processes? Is it reduced growth or is it increased mortality? Or are plants failing to flower?”.

Put another way: Cactus species could be suffering from sublethal effects of drought—the lack of water may not kill them immediately but still interfere with their reproduction. And without procreation, populations shrink. In the Sonoran Desert, for example, the iconic, towering saguaro is reproducing less frequently during prolonged droughts, because they’re not always flowering.

Part of the problem is that while desert ecosystems may look tough and hardened overall, they’re in fact quite fragile. “They don’t recover quickly once they are disturbed, or once they are degraded, just because there’s so little water and such higher temperatures,” stated Marcos Robles, the Nature Conservancy’s lead scientist in Arizona, who wasn’t involved in the research. “It takes multiple decades to recover, and in some cases, they don’t recover.”.

Now drought is compounding with other stressors, like habitat loss and poaching, to drive cactus species to the brink. On the opposite end of the spectrum, too much water from rising seas has led to the local extinction of the Key Largo tree cactus in Florida. Higher nighttime temperatures are a problem, too, since that’s when saguaro and other cacti respire to avoid water loss. “If the nighttime temperatures are very high, they can’t do it,” said Rod Haenni, president of the nonprofit Cactus and Succulent Society of America, who wasn’t involved in the paper. “Then if you water on top of that, the plants just rot.”.

Sources indicate that still, some cactus species may persist by picking up and leaving. Well, not individual plants, but populations could creep into areas with climates that more closely match what the species adapted to all those millennia ago. A similar thing is happening in the Arctic, actually, where some plants are marching farther north in “search” of increasingly agreeable weather and more water.

In a recent development, in the end, the purpose of this sort of research is to better inform where conservation professionals might concentrate their efforts. They can’t stop droughts, of course, but they can evacuate enough cacti to establish populations in botanical gardens, where they can be cared for as the climate grows more unrecognizable to even the hardiest desert vegetation. Species can even be reintroduced to the wild in some cases, so long as drought and high temperatures don’t remain a threat to a population.

Governments, too, need to protect the lands where cacti are threatened, Haenni said. This new research, he added, could make that happen. “Hopefully, it’ll wake people up,” Haenni said. “But the problem is that a lot of the governments involved are indifferent to this. There are a lot of people in the countries who are not indifferent, but they’re not running the governments.”.
