  • The change at the moment rolling out to Gemini will separate your chat history from training, which allows you to retain basic AI functionality without handing Google quite so much control over your data.
  • According to recent reports, as the new settings roll out, people will see separate toggles for model training and chat history.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that it can be hard to use Google products—or even the Internet itself—without coming into contact with Gemini AI. That is, of course, by design. The more you use Gemini, the more data Google collects to train and improve its generative AI chatbot. The options to limit training on your data have been scant, but Google is finally adding more controls to the Gemini app.

The change at the moment rolling out to Gemini will separate your chat history from training, which allows you to retain basic AI functionality without handing Google quite so much control over your data. Prior to this change, the only way to opt out of contributing your chat contents (including attachments) for training was to turn off your history. That made it impossible to go back and pick up a chat down the road.

According to recent reports, as the new settings roll out, people will see separate toggles for model training and chat history. The history setting will continue to support an adjustable limit, but you will no longer have to change it to stop Google from training on your Gemini usage. Meanwhile, AI training will be an opt-out situation. You’ll need to find the toggle to block Google from using your chats for AI training. It’s under Activity in the Gemini settings. You can also access the menu from your Google account under Data and Privacy > My Activity > Other activity > Gemini Apps Activity.

Importantly, this only applies to future interactions with Gemini. If you have previously fed any data into Google’s AI, that’s already part of the training data. That can include your inputs, Gemini’s responses, uploaded files, tool calls, your location, and data from connected apps. Google says it may retain this data for up to three years to improve its models. The organization has previously noted it attempts to filter personal information out of the Gemini activity history, but that isn’t a guarantee.

Regardless of your chosen activity settings, you can always manually remove anything you don’t want saved on the same page with the history and training toggles. However, reviewed chats that are already part of the training data won’t be retroactively deleted. Opting out of training doesn’t mean Google won’t have your data, either. Google may use this data in other ways for up to three years—it just won’t add it to AI-training data sets.

If you want to use Gemini and not retain any data, your options are actually a bit more limited now. Currently, if you disable history, Google still retains that data for 72 hours (and 24 hours for the user). Once the updated page is live, this duration will change to two months. So you can access that data for longer, but so can Google. The tech giant claims this is to “respond to you, process your feedback, and protect Google, its people, and the public.” Essentially, Google will be more able to identify nefarious AI usage even if someone disables history.

According to recent reports, even with the longer chat retention, the ability to control AI training is still a welcome privacy improvement. Google notes the change is rolling out globally, but it may take a few weeks to show up for everyone.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that proton, the privacy-focused tech organization offering alternatives to Google’s and Microsoft’s tools for email, productivity apps, password management, cloud storage, and more, is now bringing its AI app Lumo to personal computers. On Tuesday, the company released Lumo Desktop for Windows, macOS, and Linux, allowing users to leverage its AI to carry out local tasks on their computer involving connected apps, personal files, and other automations.

The app is designed to offer owners more control over their personal data, as consumer AI technology becomes focused more on AI agents that can perform tasks on users’ behalf, rather than just answering questions.

Sources indicate that already, the major model makers, including OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and others, offer desktop tools for working with their AI technologies, but doing so invites increased risk to owners’ personal data. Only this week, for instance, Apple announced it was addressing those privacy concerns with tightened security settings for apps that request Full Disk Access, as the AI desktop apps do.

Proton’s idea is to offer a set of tools that let its AI access connected apps, files, and folders and automate tasks, but with more personalization over how much access the AI has. The tech giant notes people can choose which actions the AI agent takes that will need to request their approval, and it vows that the conversations and information from connected apps and files are never used to serve personalized ads or train AI models.

The new app includes a variety of connectors that support local tools, like file-system access, shell execution, and browser content fetching, as well as Proton’s email app and other third-party services, including Confluence, Dropbox, and Jira.

The connectors also support local and remote MCP (Model Context Protocol) servers, which AI agents use to access external data sources.

Sources indicate that proton notes that people can decide for each connector which actions Lumo can perform automatically, which require approval, and which are blocked entirely. Users can also turn individual tools off from the Tools menu in their conversation.

In addition, users can set up their own scheduled tasks, where each has its own set of permissions, in terms of what can be handled automatically and what requires approval.

In a recent development, proton notes that it uses encryption to ensure that only users can read their chat history, not the company. The company also indicates it keeps no server-side logs, and the limited data it does store is protected under Swiss privacy law. The data is hosted on European infrastructure, outside the scope of the U.S. CLOUD Act, which specifies how law enforcement agencies access data held by cloud service providers.

The organization claims it’s investing in its infrastructure, with its first Nvidia B300 cluster built on Open Compute Project (OCP) standards now going live in Switzerland.

Similar to other AI apps, Lumo’s desktop app supports Skills, which lets people save instructions, templates, and reference files that Lumo can reuse across their conversations. (An earlier version of this, Custom Lumos, will now be migrated to the Skills format.).

In October, more than one in four requests on Vercel’s AI model router went to decision models, not LLMs — an enormous share of work for a kind of computerized intelligence that was invented only a month earlier.

Decision models are LLMs that don’t produce output tokens; instead, they’re tuned to use their general intelligence to quickly and cheaply decide between preset options, while registering its level of confidence in the choice. They’re useful in production environments, where fast decisions can be more important than the reasoning provided by a slower and more expensive frontier model.

TypeSafe became a $7.5 billion firm after releasing its Jev family of models, which continues to dominate usage (at least where we can see metrics from services like OpenRouter, which handled more than 1.5 billion queries to TypeSafe’s models last week).

In a recent development, however, it’s easy to make a decision model, and dozens were released in the wake of Jev. One Amazon engineer made one in a weekend, and soon every hyperscaler had its own option. On Tuesday, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella said that his company is rolling out its decision model, Microsoft-Decision-1.1, for wide use on Azure immediately.

It has been revealed that the action is happening on both ends of the ecosystem: Sqwish, a U.K.-based AI startup behind a decision model called kettei fat, announced a $3 million pre-seed round today. Co-founders Ushnish Sengupta and Federica Freddi, who met as Cambridge grad students, had been developing small LLMs when Jev arrived on the scene, and they realized their architectures could be repurposed as a powerful decision model.

Data appears to be the key to a successful model in this category — TypeSafe CEO Diogo Almeida told TechCrunch that the hardest part of building Jev was finely calibrating its judgement using statistically-adjusted synthetic data. Sqwish, too, has been working with synthetic data, but its unique offering is built-in post-training tools that use a customer’s own data to improve the model’s judgement.

“What makes us not a commodity, I think, is the fact that we are offering our customers the same power…they can keep uploading their production data, and just the same way that we trained our models, their models can also keep getting better at the tasks that they care about,” CEO Ushnish Sengupta told TechCrunch.

Attempting to rank decision models is difficult; Sqwish’s “kettei fat” model has performed well on some benchmarks, but most builders of the technology say that the real proof is in the pudding: It’s generally simple for developers to sub in one model for another and see how it does in a specific context.

“The benchmark is more of an invitation to try the model out, and then the continual tuning is the reason why they should stick to us,” Sengupta said.

The Microsoft-Decision-1.1 model is also the result of the manufacturer carefully balancing across cost, latency, intelligence and calibration. Achint Srivastava, the engineering VP who led the project, agreed that too much focus on benchmarks is a mistake.

Sources indicate that “What we see is that ‘benchmaxed’ models fall flat under real enterprise usage very swiftly,” Srivastava wrote in an email. “Our testing underscores just how challenging it is to deliver accuracy, reliability, speed and cost efficiency in a single model. Microsoft-Decision-1.1 and Jev are among the few that perform strongly across all four dimensions, even when evaluated on ‘held out’ or unseen benchmarks reflective of real use cases.”.

For startups like Sqwish, competition with hyperscalers and frontier labs is an opportunity. Freddi predicts that future systems will see so-called System One intelligence, like decision models, working in concert with System Two intelligence, like frontier LLMs.

“Even in that future, System One is maybe a $10 billion, $50 billion [opportunity], and OpenAI is doing this IPO with you know $1 trillion, $2 trillion market value in mind,” she told TechCrunch. “This is all we care about; whereas for them, this is just a side project.”.

The process of determining the cause of an IT service outage should begin with the assumption that it was an accident, notes cybersecurity researcher Doug Madory.

As the head of internet analysis at the network security firm Infoblox, Madory notes he monitors as many as a dozen network outages a day. Only around “one in a thousand,” he says, turns out to have been caused by hackers. “One's default position should be that it isn't a cyberattack,” he says, “unless there is some concrete evidence to the contrary.”.

According to recent reports, given the rarity of cyberattacks compared to outages as a whole, it’s wise to look at other potential causes first. “Most big outages that look like attacks are self-inflicted,” notes João Tomé, the internet research editor at network data firm IPinfo. (In response to WIRED’s questions, Tomé also wrote his own guide for distinguishing outages from cyberattacks.) The usual suspects, he says, include “a bad upgrade, a configuration change, a cable cut, or a power failure.”.

It has been revealed that in the scramble to understand what’s really happening with a given outage, it can sometimes be difficult to tell which sites and services are actually down. And there can be a few reasons for this muddled picture. Sometimes, an outage occurs within a foundational service like a major cloud provider. Major downtime within Amazon Web Services, for example, can cause a cascade of other failures among the company’s customers, who are all dependent on that infrastructure.

It has been revealed that in other cases, multiple services can seem to be having outages and producing errors but are functioning normally and only having problems when they interact with an entity that’s down. This appears to have been the case in this week’s bank outage: Multiple banks appeared to be having issues, but they all seem to have been linked to certain inter-institutional transactions that involved JPMorgan Chase. Another good example of this scenario is situations where it may appear that multiple wireless carriers are struggling, but all of the failed calls actually involve one network that’s having an outage.

On top of these, there are also scenarios where one or more large services are down, and false (typically accidental or anecdotal) reports of additional outages create further confusion and opacity.

In a recent development, it can all feel overwhelming, even impossible, to attempt to parse these situations yourself—but there is a concrete place to start. If you think a website or service from a major tech company may be down, look for acknowledgment of the situation on their verified social media accounts or in the “newsroom” press release section of their websites. And keep in mind that you can find these pages and social media accounts through search engines, so you definitely don’t need to be familiar with every company’s communications strategy. Use this same approach for government agencies and other institutions.

Recent findings suggest that another concrete and vital source of information that you can find through search engines is what’s often known as “status dashboard” pages. Large digital platforms specifically offer these informational pages to provide transparency into whether different components of their service are functioning normally or dealing with issues. In a major incident, companies often thread incremental updates about system restoration on the status dashboard. Industry observers note that before you start the transfer, make sure you download your fresh password manager and create a new account. The first step in switching password managers is to export an unencrypted list of all of your logins, so you want to make sure you complete the process all in one go and delete that exported list before anyone else has a chance to see it. Also, make sure you're running through the whole transfer on a single device and don't upload the login list to any cloud storage services.

It has been revealed that exactly how you download your login list depends on which program you're using now. Look for an Export option in your current program; you'll typically find it somewhere in your Settings, Preferences or File menu. You may get a choice of which file type you want to export the list as, but CSV is the standard. With that list in hand, you can go to your new password manager, find the Import option, and upload the CSV file you created. You should now be able to view the entire imported list, where you can check to make sure that all of your passwords were transferred over, the information provided is correct and there are no duplicates. It's a good idea to check the new password manager's list against the old one's to be sure that everything made the move intact.

The final step for moving your passwords over is making sure they actually work. Using your new password manager, you should visit the sites you have logins for and check that your information autofills correctly. When you're sure everything is in order, you should then wipe the old password manager. Delete your accounts and remember to delete the CSV file you downloaded as well. There's no going back at this point, so it's key to save this step for after you've verified that the new password manager is working.

In a recent development, if you want to move passkeys, the process can be considerably more difficult. Because they rely on authentication from your device, they can't be included in the password CSV file. The FIDO Alliance is a group working on standardizing the process for transferring passkeys between password managers, and it's already implemented passkey sharing for some providers.
