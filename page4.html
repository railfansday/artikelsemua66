Given the 13 years of buildup and hype surrounding the game, that’s perhaps not surprising.

Though the series has evolved over time, Grand Theft Auto has been a pop culture behemoth since its inception in 1997, thanks to its biting satire, complex storylines and cinematic gameplay.

Industry observers note that as we get our consoles ready for Grand Theft Auto 6, CNET staffers are looking back at some standout moments from the storied gaming franchise. From the original Grand Theft Auto to the streets of San Andreas and beyond, here are some of our favorite GTA memories.

Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas is still my favorite GTA game.

I have so many memories of playing Grand Theft Auto games at an age some might call “too young.” I’d go over a friend’s house and we’d boot up Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas on his PlayStation 2 and do whatever we wanted. It wasn’t until later that I found out there were missions to accomplish. And the funniest part for me was failing the mission Wrong Side of the Tracks in San Andreas. The character Major Smoke would utter the iconic line, “All we had to do was follow the damn train, CJ!” That line is funny, goofy and a good reminder that beneath the guns and violence, these are inherently silly, unserious games.

– Zachary McAuliffe, senior writer.

I still quote the policemen from GTA London 1969 who yell, “Stop right there! Oi!” My friends and I in college would play it hotseat-style on my ancient laptop, and the cockney coppers became a meme (or “banter” as we called it back then, onions hanging from our belts as was the fashion).

– Nick Hide, managing copy editor.

Ready to boogie the night away.

I’ll say walking out of Blockbuster with a copy of GTA: Vice City as a teen whose most hard-core games before that were maybe Twisted Metal and Shivers 2 made me feel like a rock star. Tearing across the city, blowing red lights and running over onlookers was an absolute thrill, especially since my friends and I didn’t even have our learner’s permits yet.

The moment that sticks with me the most, however, was the infamous private dance scene in the Pole Position mission. I was playing over a long weekend at my best friend’s house, taking turns at the wheel, when we finally got to the scandalous part we’d read about on the internet. But instead of titillated, she and I were reduced to fits of giggles as the pixelated private dancer blockily gyrated on screen. And – sorry to our parents – after regaining composure, we immediately got to copying the unsultry choreo and teaching it to our friends. These moves would be busted out at the most opportune moments, even when we got old enough to go to real clubs. Sadly, it’s never earned us as much as a free drink.

– Faith Chihil, social producer.

Nothing notes blowing off steam like blowing up a few cars.

When GTA 5 came out in 2013, I had just graduated from college, moved back home with my parents and had no idea what to do with my life. So I waited several hours in line at my local GameStop, along with hundreds of other people, to be the first to get the game. I rushed back home and played for an irresponsible amount of time. There was something comforting about having an entire world to explore when I felt stuck in my own. Mostly, though, I made life worse for the people of Los Santos. My degree wasn’t opening many doors, but carjacking was.

Sources indicate that forget the explosion-packed heists or the bullet-riddled car chases around city streets; my favorite GTA moment is when I discovered just how vast the GTA 5 map was and how much of it I could explore on foot. Or by helicopter.

GTA 5’s map was considered massive when it launched, and while I thoroughly enjoyed playing through the game’s actual storylines, I spent just as much time (if not more) simply seeing what’s out there beyond the expected play zones.

The GTA 6 map is apparently at least twice the size of that of GTA 5, and I’m super hyped to settle into hour after hour of serene exploration of a whole upcoming landscape.

– Andrew Lanxon, editor at large.

The pros and cons of using your phone as a mobile hotspot

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Skip the questionable coffee shop Wi-Fi and bring your own internet.

According to recent reports, whenever you're out and about with your laptop and you don't have a Wi-Fi network to connect to, a mobile hotspot can save the day. Thankfully, your phone can be used to share its cellular data connection with other devices, from your laptop to your tablet to your friend's phone. On iPhones, this feature is called Personal Hotspot; on Android devices, it's usually found under Hotspot & Tethering, depending on brand.

As remote work and travel keep more and more people away from traditional office spaces and your comfy home desk, it makes sense that more people need to connect to portable internet options. Of course, hotspot devices are available to buy, but more often than not flipping a simple switch on your phone's settings makes a lot more sense. Still, as appealing as this option is, there are some trade-offs that you should consider about using your phone, from battery drain to the cost of your data strategy. It's not the end of the world by any means, but it's something that comes with both pros and cons, much like anything else in the world.

According to recent reports, there are quite a few benefits to using your phone as a mobile hotspot. First of all, it's so convenient to set up your laptop on the table of your go-to coffee shop, grab your phone and turn on the hotspot feature before swiftly connecting to it. Your phone now handles your internet sharing, as well as all the other things you use it for, like calls, texts and the endless list of apps without worrying about a second gadget to charge, update or remember to pack.

Industry observers note that using your phone as a mobile hotspot is also extremely useful because you can do this anywhere you go, whether you're connecting your own laptop or you're making it easy for your kids to play Roblox on your trip. This can also be cost-efficient because you skip the expense of a dedicated hotspot device and a potential extra data plan.

There's also another fact that should most definitely be mentioned here, and that's tied to online security. Using a mobile hotspot is a safer option because you control who connects to it. That's preferable to joining potentially unsafe open Wi-Fi networks any day of the week (and twice on Sunday). It's vital, however, that you set up a strong password so random people can't join, and to make sure your hotspot uses WPA2 or WPA3 encryption. For someone who only needs a backup connection a few times a month, these benefits often outweigh the drawbacks of a dedicated device sitting unused in that tech drawer filled with cables and random gadgets.

Industry observers note that the convenience of using your phone as a mobile hotspot does come with some downsides that matter the more you rely on it. One of the most obvious ones is that running a hotspot is quite power-intensive, so your battery will drop noticeably faster while it's active. This can be solved by swiftly refilling that battery with the help of a power bank or your laptop's charger.

Besides battery drain, it's also worth considering your data strategy limitations. If your plan has a hotspot data cap, exceeding it can dramatically slow your internet speed or cost you a lot more money at the end of the month. Of course, it all depends on what you'll be using your hotspot for because video calls and streaming will burn through that data cap promptly.

You should also be aware that sharing a mobile hotspot with multiple people may mean slower speeds for everyone. Every connected device shares that same cellular connection, so performance drops as more devices join. Just be sure to remember the risks of leaving it password-free. An unsecured hotspot is very much an open door for anyone nearby, and you never really know who poses a risk. So tighten that security with a strong password.

Recent findings suggest that in order to activate the mobile hotspot on your Android device, pull down the quick settings panel and tap on the Mobile Hotspot icon. On most phones, long-pressing on the icon will take you directly to the configuration menu where you can name the network, set up a strong password and choose the frequency band (2.4GHz or 5GHz, or 6GHz on some new phones). Depending on your phone, you may also find options for the MAC address type and an automatic shut-off timer for when no one is connected to the network. The procedure is similar on iPhones. Open Settings, then Personal Hotspot (or Cellular, then Personal Hotspot). You'll need to turn on Allow Others to Join and set a strong Wi-Fi password.

Industry observers note that for occasional use, using your phone as a hotspot is a practical option that often costs nothing extra as long as your initiative includes hotspot data. It usually saves you money, doesn't require any extra purchases and gets the job done without problem. But if you're tethering daily, streaming video or connecting multiple devices for hours at a time, the downsides pile up fast. For such situations, a dedicated hotspot device may be the best option and worth the investment. You should review your data plan and your usage patterns before deciding on the best way for you.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

STARBASE, Texas—The black and silver ship fell out of the sky, emerging from the cloud deck near the very end of its flight. Incongruously, the rocket flew horizontal until the last moment when three Raptor rocket engines ignited and it swooped into a vertical position.

Sources indicate that slowly, gently, haltingly, the Starship upper stage eased its way toward the waiting tower where two arms extended outward, awaiting expectantly. The margins are so incredibly narrow. Two nubs on either side of the rocket, each protruding only about one foot, were the only purchase the rocket would have on the arms.

Then, less than a minute after it came into view, the Starship upper stage was snagged by the launch tower, as snug as you like. And so it was all over. SpaceX, nearly a quarter of a century after its founding, had just taken a hugely significant step toward developing and optimizing a massive, rapidly reusable rocket.

This was a breathtaking feat; a brilliant technical achievement that required the best engineering minds to think through a million different, difficult problems. And on Saturday evening, following a handful of practice runs into the Indian Ocean, they did it before the entire world.

Watching Starship launch from here a little less than a day earlier, and knowing it had orbited the planet multiple times, added to the sense of unreality in seeing this spaceship return to Earth under its own power, and be captured by the tower.

It felt like stepping into the future. SpaceX has dazzled us before, by landing rockets on barges and returning humans to space with Crew Dragon. But this flight took things to another level. Starships are huge. They carry a huge amount of energy coming back from orbit. And until a few years ago, it seemed beyond fantastical to think about capturing a rocket with what are essentially outsized chopsticks.

It has been revealed that humans have built reusable spacecraft that flew into orbit before. In the 1970s, flush with confidence after the triumphant Moon landings, NASA designed and built several Space Shuttles. They had solid rocket boosters that were fished from the ocean and reused. The large orange external tank was not recovered after each flight, but the orbiters went to space for two weeks, or even longer, before returning to Earth and landing on a runway.

The shuttles were engineering marvels. But during the return to Earth they acted as gliders, not igniting their engines. And their heat shields were delicate flowers, requiring painstaking refurbishment after each flight. At the outset of the shuttle program, the space agency contemplated sending dozens of humans into space at a time, and carrying cargo at a cost as low of $25 per pound.

Reality never matched these rosy projections. The crew space capped out at seven astronauts, and the cost-per-pound was closer to $25,000. Each orbiter cost an extraordinary amount of money to build; NASA only ever built five that went to space.

SpaceX has five Starships in an advanced state of development at Starbase today; and its factory houses the components for a dozen more. The company can currently manufacture a Starship upper stage in its South Texas Starfactory every three weeks. The factory was sized to produce a ship every three days. When SpaceX founder Elon Musk discussed these build targets in an interview five years ago, he sounded mad. But he no longer does.

The Space Shuttle was nearly a fully reusable space launch system, but it fell far short of low-cost, rapid launch. Over 30 years the vehicle made 135 flights. Within a few years SpaceX aims to fly 135 times a year, then thousands of times.

Such was the significance of Saturday. SpaceX aspires to fundamentally change the relationship between humanity and the heavens. The first step is to get the entire rocket back. SpaceX has now returned both elements of a Starship rocket safely to its launch tower. It will take time to reach a high rate of success with such landings, but Saturday’s safe return offered a proof of concept.

The landings will come with increasing frequency; and so will the rolls out. Over time SpaceX will perfect the heat shield that is likely to be the most difficult aspect of rapid reuse. And eventually, as difficult as it may be to believe, what we experienced with amazement this weekend will become routine. One day, we will take this all for granted.

Who knew one of the most delightful responses to the incessant wave of AI slop would be a DIY fish tank game?

Developed by Kaleb Lechowski aka byKaleb, Drawquarium is a sweet new cozy sandbox game out on Steam that's essentially the bloomscrolling of video games. Since the trailer dropped, the game's been dubbed "anti-AI" and has seen commenters on YouTube and X delighted with the game's handcrafted feel — the "brb crying because of a fish game trailer" responses are accurate.

And according to the Lechowski's Steam description, the game is not for you if you "want action or excitement. No combat, no explosions. Think of Drawquarium as the opposite of GTA 😄.".

The premise of Drawquarium is simple: draw a fish, name it, and watch it come to life swimming in your own digital aquarium. The game is focused on unlocking your creativity and sense of lighthearted tomfoolery, as you can either use templates or come up with your own ungodly creations. Reader, I'm loving my octopus sick:.

Sources indicate that in his Steam notes, Lechowski describes why he, a 3D artist in the film industry, made Drawquarium: "My heart kept pulling me toward a small, very personal game idea. One of the reasons I started developing this game was that I wanted to bring my children’s drawings to life 💖." Can't argue with that.

In a recent development, first, you'll need to title your aquarium, then it's time to draw your first fish and name them. I tend toward themes for sandbox games — shout out to my Gilmore Girls-themed faction in Cult of the Lamb — so I've named my creatures after sea goddesses.

Recent findings suggest that drawquarium's easel mode feels like a supercharged, thoughtful, fun version of MS Paint, with advanced digital brushes and tools that easily help you craft a fish-like being — and reader, when you accidentally fill the screen with the Paint Bucket instead of one section, there's an undo button. Drawing my first fish, I took more time than I thought, adding stripes and fins and flourishes to my little Amphitrite. Once you're happy with your undersea creature, click "give me my fish" and you'll drop your little creation into the tank.

Industry observers note that watching your work – be it a marine masterpiece or a passable abstract – swim around the tank is deeply satisfying. The fish spawn as tiny wee sea bairns and grow in size (something you can also set).

Recent findings suggest that once your fish is settled, you're instantly prompted to draw them a friend, which takes you back to the creative drawing board. You can clone the fish, start again, or if you need an idea, the lightbulb at the top of the easel will give you suggestions like "draw a steampunk fish," or "draw a fish reading the newspaper." You can unlock templates as you go, and the various photo editing tools you're used to are here: clone and replicate, mirroring, and erasing.

Recent findings suggest that of course, this is a drawing game, so you don't have to contain your creativity to sea creatures like I did (but my turtle slaps). Maybe your fish look like hats, stereos, or butts, all swimming in your own cursed little fish bowl. You do you, buttfish.

Decorating your tank with plants is next, as you can sketch out plants of a wild and wonderful nature. My one tip here is to play with the opacity and brush size settings, as you can create some satisfying tendrils of varying texture. And in a sweet feature, when it comes time to feed your fish with a click, it's also up to you to draw their meal. I know it's not ideal to feed octopus babies cupcakes but this is my aquarium, my choice of fish food.

You'll need to clean the tank floor (the fish instinctively run from the vacuum), and you can gather coins from the tank to buy more templates — at this stage, the game doesn't have real in-game transactions.

Sources indicate that once you've become thoroughly locked in to making fresh types of deep sea residents, there's a wallpaper mode which pops your aquarium onto your desktop so you can sit with your creations all day. This is the real magic of the game, bringing your painstakingly crafted (or slapdash) efforts into your everyday routine.

In week after week, month after month, of news about how AI is eating creatives' lunch, Drawquarium is a quietly triumphant achievement. A stunning use of human creativity in a deeply relaxing, cosy sandbox game, it's a refreshing treat.

In a recent development, apple unveiled a latest study today with the goal of better understanding how people interact with conversational AI health tools and how useful their responses actually are. The company is conducting the Apple Health AI Usage Study in collaboration with OpenEvidence, a clinical AI platform used by healthcare professionals that generates answers based on medical information from sources like The New England Journal of Medicine. Participants will use a new iOS app to ask medical questions and then rate the responses based on criteria including accuracy, detail, and relevance.

The app is now available for download, and enrollment starts today, with Apple looking for up to 200,000 people to contribute. Participants 21 and older must be based in the US, and although the app doesn’t use Apple Intelligence, it does require an iPhone 15 Pro running iOS 26 or later.

Study participants can use the app to ask health-related questions and receive easily understandable responses with links to peer-reviewed clinical sources. According to recent reports, during enrolment, participants are given the option to share Apple HealthKit data, like their activity, sleep, or clinical lab results, so responses will include more context about their own personal health. But privacy is a priority, and Apple says it has taken measures to ensure customers’ information remains confidential and the data collected won’t be used to train AI models. The aim is to improve the design and functionality of AI-powered health tools so they can deliver relevant health information that’s easier for users to understand and act on.

“At Apple, we believe responsible innovation starts with scientific rigor and clinical grounding. Health is deeply personal, and conducting a dedicated research study is the most thoughtful way to understand how people engage with, comprehend, and evaluate health AI in everyday life. Insights from this study will help advance the broader scientific conversation and ensure that as technology evolves, we continue building trusted tools that genuinely support people wherever they are on their health journey,” said Sumbul Desai, MD, Apple’s vice president of Health and Fitness.

Wildfires occur worldwide, and they can sometimes become serious threats to people’s lives. A series of massive bushfires from 2019 to 2020 swept across southeastern Australia and destroyed thousands of homes. Recently, the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires spread widely across the city, resulting in massive damage to local communities.

Many things can cause wildfires, but humans are the only factor that can also make preventative efforts. Measuring people’s impact on fire is difficult because of the mix of human and natural influences.

According to recent reports, A team led by Jianghao Wang, a professor at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, recently published a study in Cell Reports Sustainability comparing the COVID-19 era with other years. Data from the 2020 lockdowns suggest that lower human activity at this time had the net effect of lowering average fire activity.

Recent findings suggest that fire doesn’t just happen anywhere. Although plenty of flammable materials exist in natural settings, not every place experiences wildfires. Some prairies burn almost every year, while many forests go without major fires for hundreds of years. Researchers explain these differences in terms of fire regimes, which are patterns describing when, how often, and how widely and intensely fires occur.

Sources indicate that weather is a major factor affecting fire regimes. An abundance of rain helps grow grass and trees that can become fuel for fires. Whether they do or not, however, can depend on the moisture inside plants; plants with enough moisture do not burn easily. By contrast, dry conditions make them more likely to catch fire. When a drought lasts, vegetation becomes increasingly flammable. Strong winds also accelerate fire growth and spread enormously.

Human activity often combines with weather conditions to cause fires. Even routine outdoor recreation can accidentally start a fire. More people venture outdoors during sunny, drier times of the year, increasing the risk of accidental fires when dry vegetation is more likely to be present.

Industry observers note that the double-sided role of humans in fire also adds to the difficulty. Humans do start fires, but they also put them out. Firefighters contain fires that could have gone worse if no action had been taken and can keep them from spreading. Humans can also take preventative actions, such as clearing flammable material.

Sources indicate that sometimes the line between starting fires and preventing them becomes blurred. One way to clear out flammable material is to intentionally set fires to make future fires more manageable. However, these fires are hard to distinguish from other fires through satellite monitoring.

Sources indicate that an experiment is one way to find out how humans affect wildfires. If everyone stayed home under the same weather, their impact on fires would be revealed. This is why Wang’s research team focused on the COVID-19 era. It was what’s called a “natural experiment,” in which experimental conditions arose without any action by researchers.

Wang explained: “Human activity and climate normally vary together, because seasons, farming calendars, and weather all shape both fire and human behavior. The early-2020 lockdowns produced an abrupt, large-scale drop in human mobility, and the timing differed across countries while the weather did not change in the same way. This gave us a natural experiment.”.

The researchers defined the period of restricted mobility by checking regional lockdown orders and mobile phone location data. Data from NASA and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s Suomi NPP satellite helped the team track fires. Its VIIRS sensor detects heat signatures from fires at a spatial resolution of 375 meters. Each hot spot the satellite detects counts as an active-fire detection, meaning one large fire can appear as multiple spots. However, the satellite records both wildfires and prescribed burns without distinguishing between them. As a result, the intent behind each fire remains unknown.

Recent findings suggest that in the study, detections during the 2020 restriction periods were compared with those from the same weeks in 2013–2019. Weather conditions weren’t exactly the same as in past years, which was accounted for.

Clearing app caches don’t necessarily make your Mac run faster.

Your Mac applications often store temporary files, called caches, to improve user experience. This is a recommended best practice that most developers follow to prevent apps from creating or redownloading the same files each time you use them. App caches include temporary files like image previews, downloaded content and other data that help apps launch quickly and serve content without additional processing.

Recent findings suggest that for example, Google Chrome builds its app cache as you visit different websites. It caches parts of webpages, like images, which helps speed up load times when you visit the same website next time. However, the problem with leaving application caches unchecked is that it could continue taking up storage space even when an application no longer requires the cached files.

Although your Mac may manage these temporary files, there's no guarantee when this process occurs. Moreover, problematic app caches can contribute to glitches, like displaying outdated or inaccurate content. Knowing how to clear app caches on your Mac can help resolve complications arising from an outdated cache or reclaim storage space wasted on bloating caches.

Recent findings suggest that some Mac applications allow clearing app caches from within the app itself. For example, Safari and Apple Music include the options to clear app caches in their settings menu. However, this is not the case for every Mac app out there; you'll need to manually find their caches to delete them.

Your Mac typically stores application caches in ~/library/caches. Since the library is treated as a hidden directory, your Mac may not readily reveal it in the Finder. To view the library folder: Finder > command + shift + H > command + J > check "show Library Folder." Once in the library folder, press command + J > check "Calculate all sizes" to display folder sizes alongside folder names.

In the caches folder, look for the application cache you want to clear. You can distinguish third-party apps from first-party Apple apps from the folder names and prefixes. First-party Apple apps use the "com.apple" prefix in their bundle identifier, while third-party apps don't. Open the application folder in question and delete everything inside to clear its app cache. If an app cache doesn't exist in the caches folder, you can look for it in ~/library/containers. The containers folder includes apps that run in a sandbox, so their cache is also isolated. Whether your app caches are in caches or containers, you'll need to apply the same principle for finding and deleting them.

Sources indicate that it's worth understanding that clearing app caches doesn't necessarily improve application performance unless your Mac is already running short on storage space. However, deleting app caches can help reclaim storage space and sometimes even resolve app glitches. When you launch an app after clearing its cache, you might notice the app taking longer to launch, and using it might feel sluggish. This happens because the app has to rebuild its cache all over again. However, once this process is complete, your app should feel smooth and snappy to use.

It is not recommended to indiscriminately clear app caches or build a maintenance routine that involves one. Your Mac apps rely on caches to ensure a smooth user experience. You should only ever clear app caches if you notice the app not behaving as expected or if an app is taking up too much storage space. Otherwise, it's best to leave app caches alone and let your Mac manage it at its discretion.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It’s never been easy to run a battery startup in the U.S. There have been more failures than successes over the last 20 years, and whipsawing federal policy has caused more than a few battery companies to shut shop.

Sources indicate that not all of them die, though. Our Next Energy (ONE) had some close calls, but soon it will go public via SPAC, the manufacturer exclusively told TechCrunch.

ONE says it is valued at $600 million pre-money, and it expects the merger with Wen Acquisition Corp. will bring $400 million in gross proceeds assuming no redemptions. The combined tech giant will trade on the Nasdaq under the ticker “ONEE”, and the pro forma implied equity value is $1 billion.

Over the years, ONE has raised $483 million, according to PitchBook, and at one point was valued at $1.3 billion. It has also received tens of millions of dollars in incentives tied to investments and job creation in Michigan.

Recent findings suggest that ONE is the brainchild of former Ford and Apple engineer Mujeeb Ijaz, who designed a way for electric vehicles to draw power from two batteries of different chemistries. In ONE’s first setup, it paired lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) and with a manganese-rich chemistry, which would let each chemistry play to its strengths: LFP could be used day in, day out, while the more fragile but energy-dense, manganese-rich one would work for long, infrequent road trips. An initial prototype traveled over 600 miles on a single charge.

In a recent development, but the EV market didn’t take off as promptly as anticipated, and automakers wanted to buy at lower prices (usually from China). Both of those reasons helped sink ONE’s first attempt at a Series C in late 2023. The startup had to lay off a chunk of its staff, and eventually vacated its lease for a planned $1.6 billion factory in Michigan.

It has been revealed that the rough patch led to Ijaz’s ouster as CEO in December 2023, only to have him return to the position in March 2025 after a fresh round of funding closed. (His brother’s investment firm co-led the round.).

Part of the critique aimed at ONE during Ijaz’s first tenure as CEO was that the firm spent too much time developing latest products instead of focusing on one or two, as most startups tend to do. When Ijaz came back to the helm last year, he mentioned that ONE would leave the automotive market and focus on grid-scale batteries.

Recent findings suggest that ONE isn’t alone in pivoting away from vehicle batteries. American automakers, which had similarly rosy views of EV adoption, also bet significant on battery factories and supply chains. When sales didn’t pop in the U.S. like they have in China or Europe, they had to find an outlet for that capacity, and the grid started looking like the obvious target once it started groaning under the weight of AI data centers. Both Ford and GM have announced their own energy storage products.

It has been revealed that many latest grid-scale batteries use LFP, which is safer and cheaper than those used in most EVs in the U.S., and Ijaz saw an opportunity for ONE there as well. “We go back to the origins of LFP in this country and even to some of the factories that are in China,” he remarked. Indeed, just over a decade ago Ijaz led the automotive division at A123 Systems, the manufacturer that first commercialized the chemistry.

Soon, Ijaz saw other opportunities for ONE’s batteries, including defense and locomotives.

In a recent development, the defense play isn’t surprising. Nearly every battery tech giant I talk with these days mentions defense, in part because it’s a market that Chinese companies can’t play in. Building an American supply chain means that a company can sell batteries at a premium, shielded as they are from Chinese competition. It’s a higher-margin market, though much smaller than EVs.

My recent conversation with Ijaz last week was the first time I’d heard a firm pitch rail companies as potential customers, though. Ijaz noted ONE is “finding a lot of traction in” that market. The company has built a hybrid locomotive that uses a 2.8 megawatt-hour battery, and another he calls a micro-hybrid, which uses a smaller battery to start the diesel engine faster, similar to a stop-start system on a fossil fuel car or truck.

“Our customers are seeing a 50% improvement in diesel fuel consumption, which is a big deal,” Ijaz said.

Currently, ONE has three business lines, which could be a sign of its old culture creeping back into the picture. But Ijaz noted the addition of defense and rail were driven by the bottom line: Grid-scale battery customers are also “very much like automotive customers, where they were willing to buy from China as long as the price was low enough,” he said.

“Believe me, I would have loved to have made that grid business into the single business of the company. But the customers would look and then be taken away to a Chinese option because of the pricing, and the tariffs also leading them to that conclusion,” Ijaz said. “And so we decided that we needed a more durable market, and we looked for who in the customer base valued an American battery company.”.

In a recent development, today, ONE operates a pack assembly plant in Novi, Michigan, which can put together packs from cells at a rate of 3 gigawatt-hours per year. The plant uses cells from China-based and non-China-based suppliers, as well as ONE’s own cells from a smaller factory nearby, which is currently being upgraded to make 50 megawatt-hours of batteries per year.

In its investor pitch, which TechCrunch has reviewed, ONE distills its business lines into defense and rail. Drones and submarines make up the majority of what ONE sees as a $16.2 billion market. Defense-focused data centers get lumped in there, too, contributing $4.1 billion to the defense business. (That appears to be the main outlet for grid-scale batteries.) The other line, which serves the rail industry, is expected to be worth $28.2 billion.

Both markets are sizable, but they pale in comparison to the EV sector, which is worth about $73 billion today, and could grow to $200 billion by the end of the decade, according to Grand View Research. This year, ONE expects to bring in $30 million in revenue, and hopes to make double that next year. The company said it has $200 million in binding, multi-year contracts with railroads and defense contractors.

According to recent reports, ONE hasn’t closed the door on the automotive industry, Ijaz said. “Automotive still is a race to the bottom on margins,” he said. “As time goes on, if we win an automotive contract for any purpose, we’ll take a look at it, but not at any cost.”.

Recent findings suggest that ONE’s pitch centers on the so-called foreign entity of concern (FEOC) restrictions: The U.S. government can’t buy batteries made in China or those made using minerals from China-controlled supply chains, so ONE will target them first.

But it’s a pitch that many battery companies are making today. Whether investors buy into ONE’s vision will depend on if they think the firm has parted with its tumultuous past and found a focus. It will need both to succeed in the increasingly cutthroat battery industry.
