One of the iPhone Duo‘s biggest drawbacks has just been addressed – mostly.

Unlike Apple’s other current iPhones, the iPhone Duo lacks Face ID, meaning you have to rely on the noticeably less speedy Touch ID or enter a passcode to unlock the foldable device. This would be less painful if the Duo didn’t cost $1,999, and if Face ID hadn’t long been a standard feature across the company’s other iPhones, including its most affordable option.

It has been revealed that now, there’s something of a fix. In its latest iOS 27.3 developer beta, Apple has added an option to unlock the iPhone Duo using the front camera. This should make accessing the phone’s contents more seamless and intuitive.

Recent findings suggest that until now, my least favorite aspect of using the iPhone Duo has been the inability to unlock using facial recognition – a limitation that stands in stark contrast to the foldable phone’s premium feel. Scanning a fingerprint with Touch ID not only feels antiquated for such a pricey device, but also rather slow. Samsung’s Galaxy Z Fold 8, by contrast, has a facial recognition feature, as well as fingerprint unlocking that’s much faster than the Duo’s.

It has been revealed that it appears Apple heard our cries and is bringing a workaround to the Duo. This fresh unlocking capability isn’t quite the same as Face ID, a more secure biometric facial recognition and authentication system. Instead, the iPhone Duo will now let you unlock the phone using the selfie camera under slightly more limited conditions to help ensure security.

To enable the new optional unlock feature, go to your Duo’s Settings and toggle it on under Touch ID & Passcode. Touch ID is also required when using Apple Pay or accessing your passwords.

In a recent development, unlocking with the front camera is possible because of a new capability in Apple silicon called Secure Exclaves. The iPhone Duo uses this to securely and privately process data of a user’s face and verify the match. That means iOS, apps, consumers and Apple can’t access the data used to unlock the phone via the front camera.

Sources indicate that I admittedly didn’t think this update would arrive on Apple’s first-generation foldable; I assumed we’d just have to cross our fingers and hope for Face ID to land on the next iteration. So I’m glad there’s a software update to help address that gap on the Duo.

The next iOS 27.3 developer beta also adds the option for the iPhone Duo to automatically lock when someone closes the phone. You can enable this under Settings > Display & Brightness.

Alongside other recent software updates, including the addition of Reachability to make the Duo’s cover screen content easier to access one-handed, and support for Apple Pencil with USB-C, Apple appears poised to continue rolling out features that make the Duo more adaptable.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A latest startup is taking a different approach to helping people manage their screen time. Instead of blocking apps or restricting the phone’s use after a set period, an app called Island encourages you to meet your screen time goals by way of a farming simulator game. Yes, a game! Island works by rewarding you when you’re disciplined enough to meet your goals with a “credit score” that increases your farm’s production, unlocking new crops, animals, and other content.

This carrot-instead-of-stick approach was dreamed up by entrepreneurs Ashish Selvaraj (CEO) and Ethan Pronev (CTO), who met while studying at the University of Waterloo, then went on to work at companies that included Jane Street, Citadel, Databricks, and Microsoft.

Later, they quit their jobs to join the startup accelerator Y Combinator, where they first tried out other ideas in areas like B2B and software-as-a-service, but weren’t finding the right fit.

Island ended up being their fifth pivot, and the first the founders actually found themselves enjoying.

“Once we were in YC, I think there’s a major mindset — I think in [San Francisco], in general — of, ‘selling shovels.’ Everyone wants to ‘sell shovels’ to other companies, and around us, that’s just what was going on,” explains Selvaraj, speaking about the concept that many successful startups sell the tools, infrastructure, or services to others following whatever the current market boom or trend may be.

According to recent reports, “So all the ideas we had — we tried these things — but we found that we weren’t passionate about it. Like idea after idea seemed like ultimately the product was just a dashboard with some kind of different backend,” he added. “It felt like we quit our jobs … just to do something that was a variation of that.”.

With Island, the team gets to tap into their creativity, having drawn the pixel art themselves.

“A lot of people do use [AI] models to generate the art, but we felt that it had a certain character when we drew it ourselves,” he told TechCrunch. “I feel like with Claude getting so good, there’s a tendency to give away every task to Claude. But ultimately it feels like there’s something else that comes out when you do it yourself, especially in something like art,” Selvaraj said.

What’s more, the app does appear to be successful in helping users meet their goals, the startup’s early data reveals. After early testing and an October launch, the team claims that the majority of its users (82%) will end their screen time right at the limit they’ve set. Many more are ending their screen time when they get the “15 minutes left” warning.

Sources indicate that island could appeal to those who have given up on other screen time apps out of frustration because the app doesn’t actually lock you out of apps or your phone. That’s something that can end up being more disruptive, especially when you’re in the middle of doing something, like posting, sharing, commenting, or chatting, among other things. Instead, Island taps into users’ own self-discipline and their desire to see their island flourish to encourage screen time breaks.

It has been revealed that the idea to encourage users to keep their goals by offering some other type of in-app reward system isn’t entirely novel. Last year, Hank Green’s screen time app Focus Friend went viral for its experience, which involved a little friend that could only knit items that, in turn, allowed you to get points used to decorate your room when you were focused on your non-distracting apps.

Similar ideas have been used in self-care apps and habit-trackers like Finch, Amaru, Belly, Flora, Urso, and others. But Island expands this concept from being focused on helping out a virtual friend or pet to one that’s more of a game — and one where you can experience the consequences without “having to torture your pet,” notes Selvaraj.

Recent findings suggest that island also allows users to add friends and visit each other’s islands, compete in group challenges, and keep each other accountable by tying your island together with one friend of your choosing. In the latter scenario, if either friend goes over their limit, both of their islands will suffer.

The startup says Island has proven to be fairly “sticky” with a 51% daily active people-to-monthly active users ratio, and a day 30 retention rate of 49%, meaning 49% of users were still active on the app on the 30th day since the app was installed.

According to recent reports, the app is right now free to use, with a $39.99 per year subscription that allows you to set individual limits on different apps, instead of just an overall daily screen time limit. The company hasn’t raised additional seed funding outside of YC, but plans to begin marketing the app on social media for the rest of the year.

NASA’s Voyager 1 left Earth on Sept. 5, 1977, carrying a camera, a set of science instruments and a gold-plated record of sounds and images from home. Forty-nine years later, it’s about to reach a distance no spacecraft has reached before: one light-day, or roughly 16.1 billion miles. At that range, light needs a full 24 hours to make the trip between Earth and the probe. NASA expects Voyager 1 to cross the line on Nov. 18, nearly five decades into a mission originally planned to last about five years.

It has been revealed that it’s quite an accomplishment for a spacecraft built in the mid-1970s One light-day is the distance that light travels in a single day at the speed of light, which is approximately 186,282 miles per second. That translates to 25.9 billion kilometers or about 16.1 billion miles in a single day. At an average human walking speed of 3 mph, it would take a human 612,015 years to walk that distance.

Fortunately, Voyager 1 has been traveling a bit faster than that. It right now zips along the cosmos at a steady 37,763 mph, a speed it achieved in 1980 after a gravity-assisted Saturn flyby. It has been flying at that speed ever since.

According to recent reports, the milestone comes 14 years after its previous one, when Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause, the point where the sun’s heliosphere ends, and entered interstellar space. It’s also 28 years after passing Pioneer 10 to become the human-made object that has traveled the farthest into space.

The historic milestone isn’t without its drama. Voyager 1 has been in space for nearly half a century, and in that time, NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory has done everything it can to extend the life of Voyager 1. That includes troubleshooting challenges, performing maintenance and shutting down various systems to save power and extend the spacecraft’s life-span.

According to recent reports, several of those efforts took place in 2026. The team running Voyager 1 spent weeks shutting down three devices and powering on three low-power alternatives to reduce overall power usage. This initiative, which was internally called The Major Bang, was also performed on Voyager 2. The transition was completed in early September, giving Voyager 1 more time with its dwindling power supply while also keeping it warm enough to continue operating in the frigid conditions of interstellar space.

It was a tricky maneuver that required the team to simultaneously turn multiple instruments on and off. The difficulty of the maneuver was amplified by the fact that it took almost a full day for any signal to reach Voyager and another full day for Voyager to send a signal back, with hours of downtime while the spacecraft performed the operations.

Recent findings suggest that all of this took place several months after Voyager 1 experienced a sudden drop in power, which necessitated shutting off one of its three remaining instruments still in operation. Voyager 1 now has only two instruments still in operation.

Making it a whole light-day away from Earth is likely going to be Voyager 1’s final milestone while it still has enough power to communicate with Earth. Voyager 1 is powered by three radioisotope thermoelectric generators. They are essentially nuclear-powered batteries that generate energy by converting the steady heat from decaying plutonium-238.

When Voyager 1 first released, its radioisotope thermoelectric generators produced about 470 watts of power. But plutonium-238 doesn’t last forever, and after 50 years in space, the RTGs now produce about 215 watts of power, with an average loss of about 4 watts per year. Most of that power is used to keep Voyager 1 up and running, which is why NASA has shut down eight of the 10 instruments that Voyager had when it launched.

At its current rate of usage and decay, Voyager 1 is expected to shut down its final science instrument in the 2030s and will eventually lose communication with Earth in 2036 due to insufficient power to send back a radio signal.

The same fate is expected for Voyager 2, Voyager 1’s twin, which has also spent half a century in space. While it is labeled the sequel, Voyager 2 actually launched 16 days before Voyager 1, and the two have spent nearly identical amounts of time traversing the cosmos. Thanks to its slower speed and trajectory, it likely won’t reach the one light-day distance until after its instruments shut down.

Thanks to the near-perfect vacuum of outer space, Voyager 1 will continue to fly at its current speed even after it shuts down entirely. So it’ll still hit that milestone; it just won’t be able to phone home to tell us about it.

The team started with a list of 675 facilities from a commercial database based on news reports, company statements, and satellite imagery. Relying on other research, they use a range—some conventional data centers run at an average of about 48 percent of capacity, while AI training loads have been running higher, at around 70 percent.

Recent findings suggest that putting that together, the total power usage for the year by these 403 data centers was estimated at 68 to 99 terawatt-hours. That amounts to 1.5 to 2.2 percent of total US electricity use, on par with the total used by Wisconsin or Indiana.

A recent Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report estimated the total electricity use of all data centers of any kind in the US (minus crypto mining) at 76 terawatt-hours in 2018. In 2024, that number was up to 192 terawatt-hours. That means the hyperscale data centers identified by this study account for something like one-third to one-half of all data center energy use.

Sources indicate that the researchers also wanted to estimate the greenhouse gas emissions associated with generating all that electricity. It’s fairly straightforward to get a reasonable number here—calculate the mix of generation in each data center’s region of the grid, and apply that CO2 per watt-hour rate to a data center’s electricity use.

This yields 21 to 31 million metric tons of CO2 for the year. That’s around 0.5 percent of total US fossil fuel CO2 emissions.

These data centers aren’t located in areas of especially clean energy—their energy mix was only slightly cleaner than the US average. While this may not affect this study’s numbers much, it is becoming increasingly significant over time.

This matters because the types of generators being used are much less efficient than utility power plants, emitting 60 to 80 percent more greenhouse gases to produce the same amount of electricity. (Conversely, if a data center built solar generation instead, it could reduce the carbon footprint of its energy.).

Although this study can’t pinpoint the energy use of AI data centers alone, repeating this analysis in subsequent years could reveal the growth related to the AI buildout—at least as long as that buildout lasts.

It has been revealed that hyperscale data centers are the largest and most powerful data center facilities, featuring thousands of servers to power cloud computing, model training and the global use of AI. Guidi stated he was inspired to look into data center emissions and the broader impacts of generative AI after reading a paper on bitcoin mining’s electricity use, which found that just 34 mining facilities, combined, consumed as much electricity as the entire city of Los Angeles.

The study narrowed in on 403 of these facilities to provide the most up-to-date and transparent measures of their electricity use and carbon emissions between May 2024 and April 2025.

According to recent reports, tech companies are not quick to disclose their energy use, so Guidi and his team had to circumvent these obstacles to get accurate metrics. The team used satellite imagery to narrow down 675 candidate sites to 403 verified facilities, estimated annual electricity consumption, and linked them to Environmental Protection Agency data to measure each facility’s carbon emissions.

“I would say the data preparation, data collection and validation were 100% the toughest part of the whole project,” Guidi noted during a phone interview, explaining that there is a need for transparency and people should have access to this data.

During the study, the team found that these hyperscale data centers emitted 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide, more than the entire country of Denmark and far exceeding the last peer-reviewed estimate from 2018. A majority of the electricity (54%) that powers these hyperscale data centers comes from natural gas and coal, despite many tech companies signing corporate pledges to make substantial investments in renewable energy. Fossil fuels have long been known to generate massive amounts of carbon dioxide, exacerbating global warming and climate change, despite many of these tech companies. “We need to distinguish between what companies commit to on paper, and then the actual physical emissions associated with the whole powering of these infrastructures,” Guidi said.
