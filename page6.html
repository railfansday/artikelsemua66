According to recent reports, model ML, a London startup that builds AI agents for bankers, consultants and investors, has raised $250m at a $1.5bn valuation. General Atlantic and QED Investors co-led the Series B round, the company said on Tuesday.

In a recent development, evolution Equity Partners, Airtree, Forestay Capital, Y Combinator, FT Partners, Latitude, Inception Fund and 13Books Capital also took part. The round takes Model ML’s total funding to $350m. FT Partners led its $75m Series A in November 2025, and HSBC Asset Management invested in August.

Recent findings suggest that in the third quarter, Model ML’s annual recurring revenue grew 12 times year on year and its daily active users rose 26 times, the company said. It will use the money to grow its research and engineering teams in London and Newly introduced York.

Sources indicate that brothers Chaz and Arnie Englander founded Model ML in 2023, Bloomberg reported. They previously co-founded Fat Llama, a rental marketplace that Sweden’s Hygglo bought for $41.5m in 2022, TechCrunch reported.

Each user gets an agent that runs on the firm’s own systems and data. When news breaks on a tech giant a banker covers, the agent judges whether it matters and prepares the analysis in the firm’s formats. It works in tools such as Excel and PowerPoint to build pitch decks and due diligence reports.

It has been revealed that “AI should do more than give you another answer or notification. It should understand your work and get things done,” said Chaz Englander, Model ML’s co-founder and CEO.

It has been revealed that model ML sends each task to the AI model that suits it best. A growing share runs on its own fine-tuned open-source models. On the work they handle, these cost 2% as much as frontier models and are in most cases five times faster, the company said. It added that customer data is never used to train AI models.

Sources indicate that banks have tied job cuts to AI. Barclays noted in October it would cut 5,000 jobs, and Standard Chartered plans to cut 7,800 jobs in back-office roles by 2030. Englander played down the idea that Model ML is mainly a way to cut costs.

It has been revealed that “The average age of a Model ML user is 33 years old, not 23 years old,” Englander told Bloomberg. “These tools are no longer productivity and efficiency tools. They’re revenue generative.”.

It has been revealed that revenue is split fairly evenly across banks, consulting firms and asset managers, he said. Model ML’s rivals include Latest York’s Rogo, which raised $160m at a $2bn valuation in April. OpenAI and Anthropic also sell AI tools to financial professionals.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that after closing the largest initial public offering in history on Wednesday, Anthropic has about $200 billion in the bank, more than every tech giant besides Alphabet. But the AI lab has also committed to paying vendors more than $900 billion over the next 19 years, mostly to develop data centers powering Claude and its other AI models.

Some financial analysts say that gap doesn’t look concerning when you factor in that the company’s most recent quarterly sales were $17 billion, up more than 1,100 percent from the year before. Anthropic shares, trading under the ticker symbol ANT, jumped more than 30 percent when they debuted on Nasdaq midday Thursday.

The case for pessimism over the long term rests on problems that may slow Anthropic’s progress in developing latest tools. This month, US voters largely rejected data centers when given the chance, and protests against new projects show no signs of slowing. Delays in the launch or expansion of new facilities could leave Anthropic struggling to train more powerful AI models.

“The jury’s still out on whether artificial intelligence is going to benefit everyone, or just a few people,” states Elaine Matheny, one of 70 members of Mason County United, a grassroots organization fighting an under-construction data center intended for Anthropic in West Virginia. “Do we really need to cover up all our farmland so Claude can do all our thinking for us?”.

Then there are the concerns about the safety and security of Anthropic’s models. At a livestreamed investor meeting last week, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei said developing safeguards was the limiting factor in deploying its AI models. “We don’t want these agents to run wild,” he said. It’s possible the company could prioritize safety to a point that stunts growth.

Sources indicate that the San Francisco-based developer is organized as a public benefit corporation, a legal designation that allows it to prioritize societal impacts alongside shareholder interests. Amodei has said the organization held back a new model earlier this year, jeopardizing sales to protect the public from potentially harmful technology. He also has said the company tries to screen for hires “oriented toward the public benefit” to ingrain the safety culture among its 5,300 employees, which is “painful in the short run” as the company passes over otherwise qualified candidates.

It has been revealed that anthropic’s market debut is the largest for a so-called PBC, and the first for a company solely focused on generative AI development. It also represents a triumph for the handful of renegade engineers and business leaders who started it. They siphoned themselves off from OpenAI in the midst of the pandemic nearly six years ago over strategic differences, and the two companies have been rivals ever since.

For some time, OpenAI was expected to go public first, but executive departures, shifting priorities, and legal troubles have introduced roadblocks.

It has been revealed that anthropic also has some compelling numbers to show investors. Revenue is soaring, while operating losses are declining. Its big spat with the US Department of Defense this year over limits on Claude use doesn’t appear to have significantly slowed growth. Overall, the number of customers spending six figures annually is up 12 times compared to a year ago. “I don’t think you could have dreamt of an [IPO prospectus] that’s as good,” states Tomasz Tunguz, general partner at the investment firm Theory Ventures.

Because of how Anthropic must account for compensating employees with stock, it’s still wildly unprofitable—over $104 billion in losses this year through September. That’s no matter when investors are eager to buy shares and lend money.

According to recent reports, the IPO raised about $81 billion (more than $107 billion when adding in private sales of shares), which is more than the $75 billion SpaceX received in June in a previous world record. Anthropic’s initial valuation of roughly $1.5 trillion fell short of SpaceX’s nearly $1.8 trillion, though the Elon Musk-led rocket maker does have a more sprawling business beyond AI. (OpenAI has been valued at $1.25 trillion in an ongoing fundraising campaign.).

Anthropic reportedly sought, but failed, to achieve a higher valuation, suggesting some anxiety on Wall Street. US midterm election results have increased the possibility of new regulations on emerging technologies. Fears about the debt enabling AI data center development are rising, and a potential pullback in investment from Nvidia after CEO Jensen Huang’s sudden exit are deepening those worries.

Industry observers note that amodei and OpenAI’s Sam Altman have insisted investors are bullish in the long term because demand for AI models is surging. But it’s questionable whether the companies can sustain their business without coming up with ever more powerful models that drive their profits by enabling customers to automate latest types of work. Without eye-catching capabilities, customers could shift to cheaper alternatives.

Sources indicate that some Anthropic employees have taken the safety concerns to extremes. Researcher Jacob Coxon resigned from Anthropic in September, citing a belief that humanity could be destroyed within a couple of years without even more focus on keeping models under human control. But, more recently, Coxon was quoted by the Stanford Daily as saying that he might return to Anthropic because he suspects he could have “more of an impact” working on safety there.

Sources indicate that notably, Amodei mentioned safety as a challenge even above access to electricity for data centers, which many other AI companies list as their top problem. On the ground, people opposing data centers have griped about not just higher power bills, but also construction-induced traffic and housing woes, environmental pollution, and a lack of significant financial benefit.

According to recent reports, at data center developer Nscale’s site for Anthropic in West Virginia, extreme rain combined with grading (leveling or sloping ground for construction) coincided with flooding in neighbor’s backyards. “We’re frustrated, we’re angry,” the community spokesperson Matheny says. “We want [data centers] in more logical locations and not to be considering profit as the only guideline.” (Nscale says it has fixed damage and bought out some homeowners at above-market rates.).

Environmental activists have pointed out that Anthropic has made no commitment about using renewable power at its data centers, and has never published an environmental impact report.

According to recent reports, “Clearly permitting a latest data center development will be extremely difficult in the US,” Tunguz says. Pushback could force Anthropic and others to focus outside the US for data centers. That will add risk, complexity, and cost, he says.

A handful of labor groups and others including National Nurses United urged retirement funds to steer clear of the IPO, saying they “should not be invested in companies that harm our communities and allow a handful of individuals to accumulate historic fortunes and power.”.

Sources indicate that yolanda Daniel, a member of the homeowner associations’ coalition Environmental Advocates Mississippi, indicates Anthropic is socializing the cost of AI development and privatizing the gains from it. “That is not how a public benefit corporation operates,” she says, vowing to do everything she can to stop next year’s planned opening of an Anthropic data center across the street from her.

Anthropic’s IPO paperwork argued some of its public benefit will materialize as employees and early investors become some of the world’s biggest philanthropists. But there’s no guarantee of that, or that anyone will give away their money to causes that directly benefit disgruntled communities. And if employees donate shares to one of the philanthropic funds the manufacturer offers, they also consolidate power with Amodei by handing over their voting rights to him.

Amodei and his fellow cofounders collectively hold a majority of Anthropic’s shareholder voting power. Some investor watchdogs have warned about what they view as concentrated control. But a successful debut has shown investors are entrusting Amodei and his associates with building the biggest company in the world—that is if he can find the employees and data centers to do it.

In a recent development, productivity apps are simultaneously trying to feed more context to their AI tools while also making those AI assistants available in every nook and corner of their products. Superhuman is revamping its onboarding flow to let users connect their preferred tools and load context about their work upfront.

Recent findings suggest that the assistant works inline with the cursor — just like Grammarly — and also manages other agents. Superhuman is also making the agent proactive, which means it looks for your schedule, keeps an eye on your email, and even drafts responses. The updated Go agent also learns users’ writing styles and responds accordingly in email and Docs.

The productivity tech giant is adding 20 fresh connected agents, including DeepL, Legora, Amplitude, Databricks, and Blackboard, through its Go SDK.

💡 Key Takeaways
  • It has been revealed that “AI training” usually refers to instilling artificial intelligence with human knowledge.
  • CEO Scott Keogh spoke with CNET during a reporters roundtable at Scout’s still-under-construction factory in Blythewood, South Carolina.
  • Keogh described the approach as starting from the work itself.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that “AI training” usually refers to instilling artificial intelligence with human knowledge. Scout Motors indicates it’s been doing the opposite: using AI tools to help hire, onboard and train factory workers, including an AI assistant in an earpiece that guides trainees through tasks as they perform them.

CEO Scott Keogh spoke with CNET during a reporters roundtable at Scout’s still-under-construction factory in Blythewood, South Carolina. Keogh mentioned that rather than “sitting fresh hires down with an instructional binder and a test,” workers are getting AI-driven, hands-on experience early in a push to make the training more efficient and effective. Meanwhile, the automaker can collect and feed training data back into the system to fine-tune its hiring, training and placement processes.

Keogh described the approach as starting from the work itself. Manufacturing, he said, can be broken down into a handful of fundamental tasks, and the AI system is built to teach each one. He offered parts picking as the simplest example: A worker walks to 12 boxes, pulls 12 parts, confirms they’re in the right sequence and checks them for defects. The AI in the earpiece can guide the worker through that sequence, providing real-time, adaptable instructions.

Recent findings suggest that “We can train that within minutes,” Keogh said. “A worker repeats the cycle about 15 times, getting faster and better each pass, and then moves down the task line to the next skill.”.

To my ears, that doesn’t sound like the sort of task you’d need an AI system to help with and, to some extent, Scout’s executives agreed.

However, the automaker’s representatives argued that the upcoming method is far more productive than traditional classroom training — with increasing benefits as the trained tasks and concepts become more complex. Keogh also linked the speed at which the company has seen its newest factory hires become productive with how quickly Scout can ramp up the factory itself.

Scout execs say AI also plays a role in the hiring and onboarding before training begins. Todd Johnson, vice president of manufacturing, remarked the team feeds new hires’ backgrounds into an AI system, which suggests where each person might fit best: A former forklift driver might be steered toward logistics, someone with no final assembly experience might do better in the body shop and a person with paint shop experience could be placed as a painter.

Again, that sounded a lot like common sense to me. Isn’t that how people were hired before AI? According to Scout Motors’ leadership, the algorithm can potentially identify less obvious connections between skills and tasks that a human might miss, expanding access to jobs for applicants who may not have received traditional manufacturing training, which is important because Scout’s latest plant is located in an area that doesn’t have a large pool of local dyed-in-the-wool traditional automotive workers.

According to recent reports, to fill the over 4,000 roles that factory will need to reach full speed ahead by 2028, the automaker indicates it’s hiring from a national pool of applicants. However, executives told me that using AI to consider nontraditional factory workers — citing one hire who worked at the DMV — will allow Scout to identify and place more local applicants and bring fresh ideas to the factory floor.

Ryan Decker, vice president of strategy and brand, called Scout’s AI-forward approach to placement and training “a passion project for the organization.” Decker told CNET that Scout’s training AI can tailor instruction to each person rather than delivering the same material to a room of 10 people over 10 days. People learn differently, he noted, with different language skills and levels of technical depth.

Industry observers note that decker also stated the system is bidirectional. The AI can learn from how each group responds to the training and use that information to modify instruction for the next cohort. Decker thinks that each subsequent batch of workers will be “1% better,” and that after 1,000 repetitions, he expects the AI to be “dialed in and know how to provide information to the people in the most productive way possible.”.

According to recent reports, scout notes it’s also exploring vision-based AI training, in which the trainee is guided via augmented reality glasses, as the next evolution of the technology. The automaker is also looking to eventually expand the tech for do-it-yourself use by owners at home. I imagine a future where owners can use an Apple Vision Pro or similar device — which would be a pricey investment — to guide themselves through installing a roof rack or performing routine maintenance.

In practice, the next step, according to Scout’s execs, is to scale its AI learning tools beyond the training room and into the actual factory and service centers, where the automaker believes the tech can continue to improve on-the-floor productivity and guide its workers as they advance through their careers.

It has been revealed that decker states the AI can ingest and organize decades of institutional knowledge, making it accessible to workers during and beyond the training process. Scout has been feeding the AI equipment manuals and manufacturing documentation spanning multiple decades, disciplines and countries — what Decker called “hundreds of years of accumulated enterprise know-how” — which the AI earpiece can surface in real time, something he stated was near impossible to do before in a world of binders and PDFs.

Industry observers note that scout’s AI-fueled “passion project” arrives amid broader tensions across industries affected by artificial intelligence. Companies are eager for the efficiencies AI promises, while workers worry the same technology could eventually cost them their jobs. Scout’s executives didn’t address that concern directly in this exchange. Keogh framed the payoff in terms of speed — how promptly a new hire can be made productive — leaving the wider workforce conversation for another time.

Male mice, for instance, who watched other mice fighting learned enough about how to fight to better protect themselves (compared to mice who didn’t watch fights) when pitted against stronger, more aggressive opponents, according to a paper published in the journal Nature.

Sources indicate that the team randomly assigned male mice to three groups—fighters, spectators, or novices—and then subjected each group to eight days of training with three 10-minute sessions per day. The novices played with stuffed mice in isolation, while the spectators watched various pairs of fighter mice battle from a raised platform behind a transparent barrier—a set-up chosen to let them smell and listen to the fights as well as observe them.

The ninth day was “fight night,” when all the mice had to face a daunting opponent in three 4-minute rounds. Each fight was filmed from above so that the mouse behavior could be analyzed. To monitor corresponding changes in brain activity, the team engineered the rodents’ excitatory brain cells to glow green whenever they were activated, while inhibitory brain cells glowed red. They also implanted tiny optical fibers into 12 interconnected brain regions so they could track changing activity in every brain region simultaneously.

It has been revealed that how did they get the mice to fight? “Mice are naturally territorial,” co-author Jorge Iravedra-Garcia of Princeton University told Ars. “A traditional behavioral assay that experimenters use is called resident intruder. You just drop mice that are bred to be slightly weaker, and a little bit more docile, into the home cage of strains we know are more on the aggressive side. Those strains are typically very good at just naturally evoking that behavior.”.

So the fights weren’t entirely fair. “We tip the scales in favor of having a clear winner and a clear loser by having the clear winner be at home, be bigger, and be more experienced,” co-author Annegret Falkner, also of Princeton, told Ars. “We really wanted to show that these changes are adaptive in some way. Animals don’t know what the outcome of a fight is before they enter it. So we set up a situation where the outcome is a little bit more uncertain for the mouse.”.

Recent findings suggest that the results: The spectator mice ended up being better prepared for fight night—despite being weaker, less aggressive strains—because they watched fighter mice during the training sessions. Specifically, both spectators and fighters spent less time with their backs turned toward opponents than novice mice, and were also quicker to attack. They also used similar moves. Granted, they weren’t quite as skilled as their fighter-mouse brethren; practical experience still mattered. But they learned enough through observation to perform better than the novices.

According to recent reports, that behavioral change was mirrored in the neural data: the brains of the spectator mice altered to more closely resemble the brains of the fighting mice across all 12 of the interconnected regions. During training, the spectator inhibitory cells resembled a fighter’s, while the excitatory cells were closer to those of novice mice, but that changed on fight night, when both types of cells more closely resembled those of fighting mice.

Sources indicate that “When you’re watching a fight, you’re paying attention to many things at once,” remarked Iravedra-Garcia. “You’re not just simulating that aggressive behavior in your head, you’re also trying to gauge who’s dangerous, who’s not, who’s actually involved in the fight versus ‘Do I know them? Are they novel to me?’ Witnessing aggression leaves a circuit trace that changes how an animal behaves in its next conflict, and that trace looks similar to the one left by fighting itself.”.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Arriving at public markets today, Anthropic’s IPO is poised to raise billions for the manufacturer and its investors. But the biggest surprise may be just how much of that money will end up going to supplier-investors.

According to recent reports, amazon and Google, two of Anthropic’s largest infrastructure partners, hold stakes valued around a combined $307.9 billion at the company’s updated IPO price of $83 per share. Amazon, which invested $18 billion into the AI startup, would see its 14.7% stake hit upwards of $159 billion, while Google’s 13.7% stake is valued at nearly $149 billion, according to share ownership figures disclosed in Anthropic’s IPO prospectus.

A handful of other AI infrastructure providers are also positioned to benefit. Nvidia, AMD, Microsoft, Broadcom, Micron, and TSMC have joined Google in committing $26 billion to Anthropic at its IPO price in a private placement.

In total, Google has invested $12.3 billion into Anthropic since 2023, with another promised $30 billion dependent on Anthropic meeting certain performance milestones. And with the exception of TSMC, Anthropic has declared multibillion-dollar strategic partnerships with its other infrastructure supplier investors — including Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Microsoft, and Micron — that would see Anthropic purchasing their products.

This is the circular financing that has made some observers so skeptical of the AI boom. These companies are not just investors; they’re also Anthropic’s biggest suppliers. It might look like Amazon and Google are putting billions into Anthropic, but they get that money right back when Anthropic needs to train a model or serve Claude to consumers. From afar, it looks like unprecedented revenue growth for cloud companies and unprecedented fundraising from Anthropic — but it’s just the same money passing from one pocket to another.

Industry observers note that today’s IPO marks a critical change in that financing system, the moment it might just become self-sustaining. Those investments appear to have paid off, and are now liquid. Anthropic is still operating at a net loss of tens of billions of dollars each quarter — but it will no longer be suppliers and private investors footing the bill. Now, capital will come from a mix of public-market investors and corporate bonds, neither of which have the conflicts-of-interest that caused concerns before.

And critically, the company’s latest bottom line looks pretty good.

It has been revealed that once it debuts, Anthropic is expected to have about $220 billion in cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments, including proceeds from its IPO and the $26 billion private placement. Anthropic also has the ability to pay for future commitments in stock.

Joey Brookhart, who leads the Tokenomics team at SemiAnalysis, estimates that Anthropic needs roughly $280 billion in cumulative revenue over the next four years to pay for its compute bill, which Brookhart mentioned is “achievable given where they will be next year from a revenue perspective.”.

It has been revealed that “The ownership stake is very worthwhile, and those companies no longer need any more support from [suppliers],” Brookhart told TechCrunch. “Amazon doesn’t need to prop them up anymore. The revenue is all coming from other customers.”.

That doesn’t mean the server bills are going down, though. Anthropic has already committed to spend $900 billion, primarily for compute and infrastructure, according to a regulatory filing. Certain agreements have termination provisions, but Brookhart thinks the tech giant is in a strong position to pay up.

Sources indicate that on the other side, Anthropic generated $17 billion in third-quarter 2026 revenue, putting the firm on pace for a $68 billion annualized rate for the year. If Anthropic continues to grow at its current trajectory, Brookhart believes it could easily cover the gap needed to fund its compute.

Industry observers note that gil Luria, head of technology research at D.A. Davidson, agreed.

Right now, the firm’s growth is fueled by its status at the frontier of generative AI — but if it’s overtaken by OpenAI, Google DeepMind, or even cheap open-weight models, that could change fast. “For Amazon and Google, the risk isn’t actually that high because in that scenario, we’re using other models,” Luria said. “I’m arguing we’re going to use a lot more AI. I don’t know if it’s all Anthropic’s AI…. That’s still going to come from a data center managed by Amazon, Google, and Microsoft.”.

Unlike in its predecessor though, GTA 6 has no option to replay completed story missions. That marks a major change from the previous game, which allowed players to run back finished mission from the pause menu, mostly to retry its skill challenges. (Those haven't reappeared, either.).

Sources indicate that even after more than 100 hours playing the game for his review, GamesRadar editor-in-chief Josh West mentioned that he found no replay option. Sources indicate that mission replay was first added to The Ballad of Gay Tony, the second Grand Theft Auto 4 expansion, back in 2009. GTA 6 has no gold medal objectives at all, at least as things stand now. Instead, challenges can be found in their own section of the progress menu. Entire missions can play out differently based on decisions players made earlier, sometimes without knowing they were making them. Now close that eye and open your other eye. You will notice that the thumb seems to shift its position. The size of the angular shift depends on both the distance between observation locations—the distance between your eyes—and the distance to the object. The Thursday ruling from the U.K.’s Investigatory Powers Tribunal, an independent court that allows citizens to challenge government surveillance orders, is a rare rebuke for the government, which sought to keep the encryption backdoor order a state secret.

The tribunal’s ruling rejected the Home Office’s claim that disclosing the order would be damaging to national security.

The ruling comes a year after the U.K. government reportedly demanded that Apple build a backdoor to allow U.K. authorities access to customer data anywhere in the world. Details of the so-called “technical capabilities notice,” which are issued under strict non-disclosure rules, leaked to the media, which drew ire from White House officials and U.S. lawmakers who criticized the effort, arguing that the U.K. order would put Americans’ privacy at risk.

In a recent development, california-based Apple refused the order, and blocked U.K. owners from switching on its Advanced Data Protection data-encryption feature for iCloud, rather than comply with the demand. The U.K.’s secret order was later narrowed to allow access to British nationals’ data stored in Apple’s encrypted cloud, and Apple also challenged this second order.

Recent findings suggest that prior to the ruling, Apple was legally barred from disclosing the order, even after details leaked. When reached for comment, Apple told TechCrunch that it welcomes the decision to make public the fact that the U.K. Home Office issued a technical capability notice.

Apple previously said that it has “never built a backdoor or master key” to any of its products or services and “never will.”.

A spokesperson for the U.K. Home Office, which has yet to confirm the existence of a notice, did not respond to a request for comment.

Rights group Liberty International, which brought the legal challenge on behalf of two individuals, remarked that this is the first known time that the government has been compelled to confirm the existence of a secret technical order.

A final hearing on what specific order details can be released is anticipated next year.
