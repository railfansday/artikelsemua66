Hiring managers are more suspicious than ever of AI use in applications, and it’s impacting who they consider. At the same time, they’re turning to AI to help screen more candidates — meaning those videos you’ve seen on the internet of people pranking AI interviewers aren’t going away anytime soon.

Fresh data from hiring systems firm Greenhouse found that “nine in ten US employers (90%) believe they’ve turned away real candidates because those applications seemed fake or AI-generated.” Greenhouse clarified that it doesn’t know whether employers are putting applications through AI detection tools or not.

“The survey data is self-reported, so it reflects what employers believe has happened, not a measured outcome,” Greenhouse’s Head of Voice AI Ophir Samson told ZDNET via email.

According to recent reports, employers are also reacting to AI by adjusting their hiring processes: 86% of those surveyed reported changes to account for AI’s impact. 57% of them did so by adding another human interview.

“As jobseekers rely more on AI to polish and send out their job applications, employers are finding it harder to tell who’s actually right for the role,” the report stated, echoing an ongoing push-and-pull between applicants and hiring teams. AI resume screeners automatically toss a majority of applications they deem unfit, so applicants try not to get tossed by formatting their material for the screener, which then backfires anyway.

The report reveals how a tough economy and an influx of AI hiring tools are colliding, arguably to benefit employers over applicants. Greenhouse noted that, ironically, AI tools are helping hiring managers while also making resumes untrustworthy, and applicants would rather be interviewed by AI than not at all.

“A growing number of employers are using AI not just to filter candidates out, but to have conversations with more candidates, earlier, through AI-powered voice technology,” Greenhouse said, adding that “half now use AI interviews to hear from more candidates, and 40% of those have found strong candidates they would have missed.”.

Greenhouse itself offers a voice AI screening interview tool. Samson told ZDNET that an HR professional ran 200 candidates she’d rejected through Greenhouse’s tool, and it suggested 19.

“All five of the finalists came from that group, and she ultimately hired one from the group,” Samson stated.

Sources indicate that what we don’t, and maybe can’t, know is the inverse: whether AI screeners influence hiring managers to turn away candidates they actually should have considered. At least in Greenhouse’s case, Samson noted Voice AI doesn’t make hiring decisions or “automatically advance, reject, or rank candidates.”.

“Recruiters review every output, and one recruiter owns the outcome and can explain how they reached it,” he remarked.

Greenhouse’s report surveyed 1,347 hiring managers and recruiters across the US, UK, Germany, Australia, and Ireland from late September to mid-October, and found that half of employers using voice AI “say the early-stage candidate experience has improved.”.

According to recent reports, but how swayed is that by the state of the US job market? October’s job report was better than expected, but it’s still been a tough year overall for job-seekers. Greenhouse found that while employers are using AI to screen more candidates, most are maintaining human oversight over the most important decisions in the hiring process.

“Of all the tasks employers would hand to an AI agent, deciding which candidates to advance came last, at just 7%,” Greenhouse noted.

A recent California law aims to protect employees from being fired by AI systems that make determinations about their performance without a human co-signing the assessment. Way back in the age of MySpace and AIM — aka 2005 — the National Retail Federation debuted the term "Cyber Monday" after realizing that the Monday after Thanksgiving saw a flood of online sales. That, of course, and the fact that online retailers wanted some of that holiday shopping bread, too. 📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Google wants Android apps to do more than simply stretch across a bigger screen. Starting in April 2027, developers will have to meet new adaptive quality standards to keep their apps eligible for publishing and updates on Google Play.

In a recent development, the move adds to the pressure on developers to take foldable phones seriously and not to treat their larger displays as an afterthought. The newly introduced rules establish Tier 3, or Adaptive Ready, as the minimum requirement for standard apps. That means apps have to use the available screen without letterboxing or compatibility modes.

Google's announcement also says those apps must keep vital information like scroll positions, text inputs, and media playback when consumers rotate, resize, fold, or unfold their devices. Support for multi-window environments is also mandatory.

According to recent reports, the tech giant is also working to make store listings do more. Developers may already upload dedicated foldable screenshots in the Play Console, but from April 2027 fresh apps and app updates will be required to upload three foldable screenshots, on top of the usual two phone screenshots. These should be of the app running on different foldable screen configurations: square 1:1 inner displays, 4:3 inner displays, and folded outer screens.

Apart from basic compatibility, Google has two higher-quality tiers for developers willing to put in extra work. Tier 2, or Adaptive Optimized, includes support for accessories like keyboards, mice, trackpads, and styluses and responsive multi-pane layouts. Tier 1, or Adaptive Differentiated, goes even further, adding support for tabletop and book postures, camera continuity, interactive picture-in-picture, and multiple app instances.

In a recent development, there’s something for developers in it too. Apps that reach Tier 1 or Tier 2 are eligible for premium Play Store badges, improved merchandising, and editorial features. In essence, Google is trading more visibility for apps that make better use of expensive hardware.

Games have a slightly different timeline and expectations. Foldable screenshots become mandatory in April, and its Tier 3 requirements kick in starting in August 2027. Higher tiers will also consider graphics quality, performance, controller support, and frame-rate stability.

Within hours of being injected with insulin that was meant for her roommate, 93-year-old Emma Lewis’ blood sugar plummeted, and she was rushed to a nearby medical center.

According to recent reports, felicia Kaye, the licensed practical nurse who gave the injection in May 2022, was not an employee of Peachtree Centre, where Lewis lived. She was a gig worker who picked up her shift on the nursing app ShiftKey. She wasn’t familiar with Lewis or her roommate and never received orientation on any of the facility’s policies and procedures.

“It’s pretty much you get the keys [to the medication cart] and just figure it out yourself,” Kaye testified this August. Kaye declined to comment for this story.

Gig workers are flooding America’s understaffed nursing homes, resulting in a revolving door of staff caring for the nation’s elderly and infirm. ShiftKey boasts a network of more than 10,000 facilities—many of them nursing homes—and more than 200,000 health care workers hoping to fill shifts.

An investigation by Proof News and WIRED found that the gig nursing model is putting lives at risk: Numerous court filings, federal inspection reports, and interviews with former employees of the company, attorneys, and gig nurses themselves describe how ShiftKey thrusts workers into understaffed nursing homes with little to no onboarding—leading to dangerous situations.

Sources indicate that in January, a federal surveyor from the US Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services noted that a ShiftKey worker made a mistake inserting a PICC line, a catheter threaded in a patient's arm to their heart. No one at the nursing home had checked that they were trained on the procedure. In 2025, a CMS surveyor wrote that a Texas nursing home didn’t orient temporary workers on how to feed residents before a ShiftKey worker tried to force feed one with a syringe. Later that year, a nursing home in Texas banned a ShiftKey worker after a resident who was supposed to be under her care was found sitting in urine and feces.

Inspection reports also reveal alleged abuse and theft. This summer at Hillsboro Rehab in Illinois, a ShiftKey worker allegedly stole the debit cards of two residents and used one of them at a local smoke shop and ice cream store.

It has been revealed that according to the inspection report, the nursing home notified ShiftKey about the incident and blocked the worker in the app so they couldn’t pick up shifts there in the future. The worker was later arrested, and residents repaid, according to Tim Blythe, regional vice president of operations for Tutera, Hillsboro Rehab’s parent company.

Recent findings suggest that “We sincerely regret this unfortunate incident,” Blythe wrote in an email. “We completed an internal investigation and notified the individual's employer, law enforcement, the Office of Inspector General, and appropriate regulatory agencies.”.

Industry observers note that one ShiftKey worker was accused of slapping a resident in the face in Texas in 2022, and another of “verbal abuse” of a resident in Tennessee in 2023. At the Center at Grande in Texas, a third was accused of stealing hundreds of dollars from four residents in February 2025. None of the facilities responded to a request for comment.

ShiftKey pitches itself as the Uber for nursing. “We applied technology to the nurse staffing industry,” reads an internal “selling points” memo obtained by Proof and WIRED. “What Uber did was connect the customer directly with drivers. ShiftKey connects facilities directly with providers.”.

“Our technology is part of the solution to [the] patient care crisis by allowing highly experienced nurses to work on the schedules they choose and by enabling healthcare facilities to swiftly fill care gaps and deliver patient care,” ShiftKey CEO Mike Vitek stated in a statement to Proof News and WIRED.

Though ShiftKey advertises open shifts for a variety of facilities—including those for addiction treatment, psychiatric hospitals, and correctional institutions —a Proof News and WIRED analysis of 108,706 available shifts collected in March, July, and August 2026 found that 89 percent of open shifts were at nursing homes.

Sources indicate that the app offers shifts for mostly lower-paid nursing staff. A majority of the open shifts Proof Upcoming and WIRED reviewed called for licensed practical nurses or certified nursing assistants, careers that don’t require a bachelor’s degree and are paid between $20 and $30 an hour on average, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

In his statement, Vitek disputed that the model is “harmful to the industry” given “long-standing, systemic challenges within the healthcare ecosystem.” He emphasized that ShiftKey verifies credentials, conducts background checks, checks abuse registries, and makes that information available to facilities.

Recent findings suggest that “Importantly, training or orientation required by any facility to operate within its policies is conducted by that individual facility,” Vitek said. “At ShiftKey, we do not employ nurses—we simply enable their access to flexible shifts.”.

Like a growing number of platforms built to automate workforce management, ShiftKey relies on a variety of automation tools. The software allows facilities the option to automatically post and accept shifts; the app uses a “formula-based calculation” to assign workers a “reliability score,” according to Vitek, which nurses said affects their ability to get shifts; and it offers shift scoring, last-minute pricing, and dynamic pricing based on demand and supply, according to the company’s job postings and employees’ professional profiles.

But when a concern is raised, Vitek noted, a human reviews it. He said they evaluate claims of “medical malpractice or abuse, neglect, or exploitation of a patient as soon as ShiftKey receives it” and decide if deactivating the worker is “appropriate.” He declined to comment on these specific events, citing reasons of privacy and ongoing litigation. He added there may be instances where ShiftKey is not promptly notified, which can delay action.

Recent findings suggest that founded in 2016 in Texas, ShiftKey grew rapidly during the COVID-19 pandemic, as unprepared nursing homes reeled from rolling outbreaks that could claim dozens of lives in a single facility. Health care workers sought higher pay as their risks mounted, and homes scrambled to fill shifts.

Recent findings suggest that backed by private equity, ShiftKey and its competitors Clipboard Health and IntelyCare benefited. According to an analysis by the US Department of Health and Human Services, from 2019 to 2022, the use of contract staff in nursing homes tripled from 4 to 12 percent of all nursing hours. Though it’s since declined, the rate remains higher than prepandemic.

In 2023, ShiftKey’s then CEO told Axios that the firm was valued at more than $2 billion.

Nursing homes pay ShiftKey set rates for workers. The platform then “mathematically” calculates a “suggested” rate for each shift posted, Vitek noted in a statement, emphasizing that dynamic pricing tools are only used to increase pay for “difficult-to-fill” shifts—and never to decrease rates.

According to recent reports, workers then bid to claim it. This bidding system is what got Smith hooked: Instead of accepting whatever wage a facility would offer, Smith haggled for higher pay.
