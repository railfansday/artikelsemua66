📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that by Wednesday of this week—six days after a 4.5-hour nationwide ground stop that panicked onlookers and scrambled air travel—US transportation secretary Sean Duffy remarked he still wasn’t quite sure what led to the Friday meltdown. “The root cause of what happened, we don’t have clarity yet,” he said after a meeting with lawmakers.

Still, the Federal Aviation Administration has revealed that a few things did go wrong on Friday afternoon. It involved routine infrastructure replacement, a faulty router, data overload, and disruption in some regions in the radio communications that air traffic controllers use to speak to each other and pilots.

The agency has since described the ground stop as something executed “out of an abundance of caution.” On Wednesday, FAA administrator Bryan Bedford appeared to downplay the incident even as he took blame for it, calling the outage “a knee scrape, and I’m sorry.”.

While aviation industry workers maintain that flyers were safe throughout the ground stop, some felt government officials downplayed the event, which left air traffic controllers scrambling to backup frequencies even as they tried to figure out what was going on.

“That was the hairiest 90 minutes of my career,” one air traffic controller told WIRED.

Contacted for comment, FAA spokesperson Steve Kulm didn't answer questions but referred WIRED to the agency’s ground stop Q&A web page, where the agency writes it has “clear, established, standard procedures to ensure safety during incidents such as this.” It says that some radio frequencies and radar feeds remained online during the communication issues, “allowing air traffic controllers to maintain communication with and track aircraft.”.

Here’s what we know about the ground stop so far.

To understand the communications issues that led to the ground stop, here’s a quick overview on how air traffic control works.

Most flyers are familiar with the towers near airports, officially called the Airport Traffic Control Towers (ATCT). The controllers who work here manage aircraft ground movements, including takeoffs and landings. Once an aircraft gets up into the sky, around 10,000 feet, it’s passed to controllers in a Terminal Radar Approach Control (TRACON) center, who manage airport arrivals and departures. Then, once a flight is really underway, an aircraft is passed to Air Route Traffic Control Centers, often just called Centers. There are 22 of these in the US, and controllers there guide airplanes for most of their flight, passing off the aircraft as they move between regions.

The communication radios used at Centers have a range of around 150 miles—a problem when the areas they have to manage are more than 100,000 square miles each. So phone lines at the Centers lead to networks of transmitters, each installed remotely.

Industry observers note that for decades, this network has been connected by copper telecommunications lines—outdated and sometimes brittle tech. So in 2025’s GOP-led Big Beautiful Bill, the Transportation Department received the billions of dollars in funding needed to upgrade that copper to fiber-optic cables. The upgrade swaps the system, the private communications backbone of Air Traffic Control, from one of mechanical switches to a digital system built on internet-based protocols. A contractor called L3Harris—the network owner—manages the transition, and as of last week, the project was about 70 percent complete. This was where the FAA ran into problems.

In a recent development, the trouble began in El Dorado, Arkansas, the site of one of those remote communications facilities, which is part of the larger Center based in Memphis, Tennessee. There, the FAA claims a contractor with L3Harris was making on Friday what is now a routine switch from that legacy copper system to a fiber-optic one. But something, it appears, went wrong when the worker plugged in a upcoming router as part of that transition, and the FAA still doesn’t know why.

Data began to flood the network, making 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds, according to the FAA. The agency states another remote site, a radar-based facility in Amarillo, Texas, may have also flooded the network with data. To compensate, the network tried to recruit other nodes, and they got overloaded, too, taking down several radio frequencies on the network.

At first, the issue manifested as intermittent dropouts on radio frequencies at Centers in the region, from Kansas City to Chicago, according to two people familiar with the matter who spoke to WIRED under the condition of anonymity because they are not authorized to speak to the press. This made it difficult, if not impossible, for air traffic controllers to talk between Centers and with pilots through normal channels. From there, though, the issues seem to have cascaded to other regions, including the areas around Washington, DC, and New York.

Sources indicate that in control rooms—mostly Centers dealing with en route flights—air traffic controllers first noticed some “dropouts” on their radio transmissions, according to two sources. On their own, these sorts of communication disruptions aren’t unheard of. But when they began to spread to multiple regions, FAA officials really began to pay attention.

Because officials didn’t understand why the radio frequencies were unstable, the agency called a nationwide ground stop.

Recent findings suggest that one person who spoke with WIRED noted they still had access to a backup frequency during the network instability. The FAA said “some frequencies and radar feeds remained reliable.”.

Then, according to the FAA, contractors from L3 Harris blocked data from the remote Arkansas and Texas locations from the wider network. That restabilized the system, restoring radio contact between everyone in the airspace.

In a statement Friday, a spokesperson for the National Air Traffic Controllers Association, a union that represents almost 20,000 controllers, wrote that “air traffic controllers were on the job, carrying out their responsibilities and working to ensure the safety of the National Airspace System.”.

Recent findings suggest that while the technical challenges led to several uncomfortable hours for aviation professionals, midair passengers remained safe during the ground stop, says Scott Miller, an airline pilot and a lecturer in the Aviation and Technology program at San Jose State University in California. “It was certainly an abnormal situation, but it wasn’t an emergency, because we’ve trained for it,” he says. “It was not dangerous, but it was highly inconveniencing, and it was not normal.”.

Industry observers note that it helped that the radio outage did not seem to have affected all Centers, and even fewer TRACONs and local towers. That means pilots were generally in contact with controllers, especially as they circled airports and landed their planes.

Industry observers note that it also helped that pilots don’t actually need air traffic control to fly their planes, Miller claims. “Talking to a controller is not what keeps an airplane in the sky,” he says. Pilots have plenty of systems onboard that allow them to “see” what’s going on around them.

In a recent development, despite the lack of clarity on what exactly happened with the newly installed router to make it malfunction, Duffy has settled on a solution: more money. On Wednesday, he officially asked Congress for $50 billion, with $30 billion of that dedicated to air traffic control modernization.

Their improbable, sequential timing left Americans anxiously doomscrolling for signs of foul play. But in cases like these, it’s the job of cybersecurity analysts (and reporters at WIRED) to determine—vibes aside—whether the latest tech outage was actually an accident or the result of a cyberattack.

The baseline for that fact-finding, as anyone tasked with investigating these incidents will tell you, is that the vast majority of these digital breakdowns are not the result of malicious intent but rather casualties of IT admins’ constant war to keep computer systems from collapsing under the weight of their own complexity.

“I’ve just seen enough times where we thought maybe it’s a cyberattack and it turns out it’s not. In a recent development, so in the interest of soothing the country’s rattled nerves and helping us all find ground truth amid seemingly endless tech catastrophes, we at WIRED present our general guide to determining whether a tech outage is the result of a cyberattack. So take a breath—maybe open the bunker door to let in some fresh air—and read on.

The process of determining the cause of an IT service outage should begin with the assumption that it was an accident, indicates cybersecurity researcher Doug Madory.

In a recent development, as the head of Internet analysis at the network security firm Infoblox, Madory indicates he monitors as many as a dozen network outages a day. Only around “one in a thousand,” he says, turns out to have been caused by hackers. “One’s default position should be that it isn’t a cyberattack,” he says, “unless there is some concrete evidence to the contrary.”.

It has been revealed that given the rarity of cyberattacks compared to outages as a whole, it’s wise to look at other potential causes first. “Most major outages that look like attacks are self-inflicted,” says João Tomé, the Internet research editor at network data company IPinfo. (In response to WIRED’s questions, Tomé also wrote his own guide for distinguishing outages from cyberattacks.) The usual suspects, he says, include “a bad enhancement, a configuration change, a cable cut, or a power failure.”.

In the scramble to understand what’s really happening with a given outage, it can sometimes be difficult to tell which sites and services are actually down. And there can be a few reasons for this muddled picture. Sometimes, an outage occurs within a foundational service like a major cloud provider. Major downtime within Amazon Web Services, for example, can cause a cascade of other failures among the company’s customers, who are all dependent on that infrastructure.

In other cases, multiple services can seem to be having outages and producing errors but are functioning normally and only having problems when they interact with an entity that’s down. This appears to have been the case in this week’s bank outage: Multiple banks appeared to be having challenges, but they all seem to have been linked to certain inter-institutional transactions that involved JPMorgan Chase. Another good example of this scenario is situations where it may appear that multiple wireless carriers are struggling, but all of the failed calls actually involve one network that’s having an outage.

On top of these, there are also scenarios where one or more large services are down, and false (typically accidental or anecdotal) reports of additional outages create further confusion and opacity.

It can all feel overwhelming, even impossible, to attempt to parse these situations yourself—but there is a concrete place to start. If you think a website or service from a major tech firm may be down, look for acknowledgment of the situation on their verified social media accounts or in the “newsroom” press release section of their websites. And keep in mind that you can find these pages and social media accounts through search engines, so you definitely don’t need to be familiar with every company’s communications strategy. Use this same approach for government agencies and other institutions.

Another concrete and vital source of information that you can find through search engines is what’s often known as “status dashboard” pages. Large digital platforms specifically offer these informational pages to provide transparency into whether different components of their service are functioning normally or dealing with challenges. In a major incident, companies often thread incremental updates about system restoration on the status dashboard. AWS, Google Cloud Platform, and Microsoft Azure all offer these landing pages, and there are similar ones for other services like Meta business products, Google Workspace, telecom companies, AI companies, and so on.

When a cascading tech failure causes outages across many services at once, and the point of failure isn’t clear, sources of network data that monitor the Internet at a broad scale can also help determine the underlying glitch. Amanda Meng, a research scientist at Georgia Tech who helps run its Internet Outage Detection and Analysis service, or IODA, points to not only IODA’s own data—and its social media accounts on BlueSky and Mastodon—but also tools like Cloudflare Radar and Watch the Net. Infoblox’s Madory also posts his own findings about network outages on his social media accounts.

According to recent reports, when IODA sees evidence of a network disruption, “we’ll look at the timing of it, the start, the end, the network affected, anything we can find out about it, and then try and connect it with what we see from reporting,” Meng states. That scope, location, and timing of an outage can help determine a suspected cause, whether it’s a single network that might be the victim of a buggy patch, a region cut off by damage to a fiber optic cable or, in rarer instances, a cyberattack or even a government purposefully cutting off Internet access to its citizens to stifle protests.

Even with those network clues, however, Meng advises returning to step two: Once an affected service is identified, its actual owners are often the best source to monitor for updates. “Usually the operators have the most information about what’s actually going on,” she notes.

Most often, mea culpa updates from companies or government agencies confirm an outage is caused by a tech mishap. In the rarer cases when that downtime results from an actual cyberattack or hacker breach, however, the hackers responsible often claim responsibility in some form.

Ransomware gangs, perhaps the most common source of serious malicious Internet disruption, publicize their breaches on dark web pages that list their targets for the purposes of extortion, sometimes with a countdown timer designed to pressure victims to pay before they leak stolen information. In other cases, like the NotPetya or WannaCry worms—two of the worst cyberattacks in history—the hackers’ self-perpetuating code left behind ransom messages on machines that quickly spread on social media as the attacks unfolded. Ideological “hacktivist” groups—or state-sponsored hackers working for military or intelligence agencies but posing as hacktivists—also often take credit for their cyberattacks on platforms like X, Telegram, or their own websites. These messages then quickly spread to social media and through news reports.

According to recent reports, even these statements, however, should be taken as just another significant data point: Hacker groups exaggerate, misdirect, or lie when it serves their purposes. And some significant mass hacking incidents—such as the Mirai attack on the Internet infrastructure tech giant Dyn in 2016 that took down thousands of websites—have been accompanied by no public message at all from the hackers responsible.

Industry observers note that in cases where neither the perpetrator of a hacking incident nor its target has confirmed the attack, confirmation can sometimes come first from a government agency—such as the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency—or even from threat intelligence researchers at private cybersecurity firms. Government and industry investigators sometimes post their findings to their websites or share them with media, but it often takes days, weeks, or even months to come to confirmed conclusions. Hence, step five.

If all these other measures of determining the cause of an outage don’t offer a clear understanding of the situation, the best approach may be patience. “It’s going to get a little conclusive at some point,” Madory says. “Sometimes you just have to just sit and wait.”.

Industry observers note that aside from the time it takes for companies to admit to their screwups—or hackers to claim a win—the length of an outage can also sometimes offer a clue to its cause. A faulty update, power outage, or cascading bug can often be resolved promptly. Determined hackers who have maximized their damage, who shift tactics to outsmart defenders, or who may still be in a network they’ve breached can cause far longer and more severe outages. “A rollback takes minutes or hours,” Tomé says, referring to reverting to older code as a fix for a faulty update that introduces a bug. “Ransomware takes days or sometimes even weeks.”.

In other words, as with all breaking news, when facts are in short supply, it’s better to maintain skepticism about early rumors or conspiracy theories and wait for more concrete evidence.

In the information vacuum that precedes that evidence, it can be easy to see connections between events that may just be coincidences. What are the odds, you may ask, that five major IT outages occurred on a sequential Thursday, Friday, Monday, and Tuesday?

Industry observers note that for Doug Madory, who tracks those network outages every day of the week, that string of failures may just be a sign of the increasing complexity of technology—and how fundamentally our civilization has come to depend on it. “For better or worse, modern society is completely mediated through Internet-based services. They have outages from time to time. And that’s a reality I don’t think we’ll ever completely eliminate,” Madory indicates.

Industry observers note that “If you’re immersed in that world of looking at outages for years on end, then they don’t really surprise you,” he adds. “You just try to understand what’s different about each one. And maybe what we can learn from it.”.

NASA recently announced a significant shakeup to the Artemis program that will land humans on the Moon.

Recent findings suggest that the moves were designed to give the agency’s lunar lander providers—SpaceX with its Starship vehicle, and Blue Origin with Blue Moon—more flexibility in reaching the Moon. For Starship, this means docking with Orion in Earth orbit, and then traveling to the Moon in tandem. For Blue Moon, it shifts the rendezvous with Orion all the way to low-lunar orbit.

Sources indicate that these changes benefit the lunar landers, but in turn impose more of a burden on the Orion spacecraft, which is built for NASA by Lockheed Martin. To understand the implications for the spacecraft, Ars spoke with Kirk Shireman, Human Spaceflight Exploration and Orion program manager at Lockheed.

For Starship, the evolved plan has SpaceX fueling a Starship lunar lander in low-Earth orbit before docking with Orion in high-Earth orbit. The crew will fly to the Moon on board Orion before two of the four astronauts transfer into Starship for a landing on the Moon.

Due to the docking configuration with Starship, Orion must now fly backward to the Moon, with its Service Module at the top of the stack. This is a fundamentally different flying posture than Orion was designed for, Shireman stated, and creates concerns for Orion’s propulsion system.

Sources indicate that as a result, the Service Module, which is provided by the European Space Agency, will require modifications to its propellant tanks. This is because liquid propellant would move toward the gas inlet side of the tanks, rather than settling in the direction expected during normal operation.

Sources indicate that “When you push it the other way, the liquid goes to the gas end of the tank, and you don’t want the liquid in the gas part,” Shireman mentioned. “Bad things can happen to you.” To address this, he said, the European Space Agency and its main contractor, Airbus, are modifying the vehicle’s tanks for Starship lunar missions.

NASA and Lockheed engineers have also been studying the impact of increased acceleration on Orion when the vehicle is attached to Starship. Under Orion’s original mission architecture, the spacecraft would experience accelerations of about 0.1 to 0.15 g during the translunar injection burn, Shireman noted. But with Starship providing the thrust, the acceleration would increase to approximately 0.8 to 1 g, acting in the opposite direction from the one originally planned.

Orion’s structure must be certified to handle a substantially higher acceleration in the opposite direction, Shireman noted.

In a recent development, before astronauts land on the Moon, NASA is also planning a mission in low-Earth orbit to test Orion’s interactions with the lunar landers. Shireman noted this change in prepares for Artemis III, announced earlier this year, has created new hazards, as Orion was not designed to spend more than a short period of time in low-Earth orbit.

Sources indicate that heating is a concern, he noted, because Orion was designed for deep space, which is colder than the thermal environment closer to Earth. For example, he said, Orion has passive thermal features designed to prevent the spacecraft from getting too cold in deep space. One of these is a silver-looking exterior tape that dissipates electrical charge and limits the radiation of heat into space. As a result, some of this tape will be removed for the Artemis III mission.

Engineers are also analyzing how to manage electrical loads to reduce heating and protect the avionics and crew cabin.

Another substantial change that stems from spending approximately two weeks in low-Earth orbit is the orbital debris environment, which is more congested than in deep space. Shireman said NASA and Lockheed have not completed their assessment of the orbital-debris risk associated with the fresh mission profile. This should be done by January, and, if the risks are too high, it might lead to modifications of Orion’s orbit for the mission.

“There’s going to be a higher risk from an MMOD standpoint,” Shireman said of orbital debris near Earth. “It’ll be higher risk than flying out to the Moon.”.

Earlier this year, NASA removed the lunar landing from Artemis III, turning the mission into a demonstration of rendezvous and docking operations in low-Earth orbit. The agency is now targeting 2027 for the mission, leaving Orion and the lunar landing companies with a compressed schedule to complete substantial technical integration work.

It has been revealed that this may seem like a fairly straightforward process, but docking is rather complex. It involves mechanical capabilities like hooks, connectors, and pins; there are thruster-plume interactions with the landers, including possible damage from hot or abrasive exhaust; concerns about whether Orion’s laser system might incorrectly interpret reflections from surfaces on Starship or Blue Moon; and more.

Lockheed is working closely with NASA to prepare Orion to maintain an Artemis III schedule for a launch in summer or fall of 2027.

“We’re working three shifts,” Shireman remarked. “We had a zero-margin schedule already, so we’re, I call it hand-to-hand combat. Literally every day we’re clawing to see, ‘Hey, can we save a shift here? Can we save half a shift there?’”.

“We also have a responsibility to not go too fast and cut too many corners and make sure that we are not taking undue risks,” he stated.
